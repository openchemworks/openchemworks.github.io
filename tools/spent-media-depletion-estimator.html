Short answer

Free L-glutamine at 37 °C decomposes chemically into pyroglutamate and ammonia at roughly 10% per day even without cells. Select a basal medium or enter your own starting mM, viable cell density, and specific uptake rates (qAA) below to see when glutamine, asparagine, or a limiting essential amino acid will exhaust, and how much apparent loss is chemical decay versus cell metabolism.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Why does thermal glutamine breakdown distort apparent cellular uptake rates?

At 37 °C, free L-glutamine cyclizes spontaneously to pyroglutamate and releases equimolar ammonia at roughly 10% per day. Ignoring that chemical decay attributes non-cellular glutamine loss and ammonia generation to the cells, inflating apparent specific uptake (qGln) and mis-timing fed-batch feeds.

Which amino acids usually deplete first in basal mammalian media?

In published CHO comparisons across DMEM, DMEM/F-12, RPMI-1640, and Ham's F-12, asparagine (where present) and glutamine typically reach exhaustion first, while small molar pools such as tryptophan (0.010 to 0.078 mM) and methionine shift sharply in percentage terms early in the run.

How does OpenChemWorks measure spent-media amino acids and ammonia?

AA-1 ($150 per sample) quantifies 19 free amino acids plus ammonia by AQC pre-column derivatization and HPLC. For full bioreactor time courses, RUN-1 ($1,990) covers up to 24 time points in one combined table and trend chart (about $83 per sample), with overnight turnaround available on request.

Does AA-1 detect alanyl-glutamine (GlutaMAX) or cystine?

AA-1 reports free glutamine, free alanine, and free ammonia; intact alanyl-glutamine dipeptide is not counted until cleaved. Cysteine and cystine are outside the standard AA-1 panel and require the AA-3 assay.
