Applications · Protein bars and shakes

Amino acid analysis for protein bars and shakes

A protein bar or shake is a finished food, not an ingredient. If you formulate, co-manufacture, or buy these products, the question that matters is what the amino acid profile looks like after baking, binding, flavoring, and shelf storage — not what the protein powder looked like going in. Brands and co-manufacturers send us finished bars and shakes to verify a profile, compare production runs, or check a supplier change. The result feeds a release, reformulation, or supplier decision.

What this sample type is

Bars and shakes combine protein with fats, sugars or sugar alcohols, fibers, flavors, and binders. A bar is a dense, low-moisture solid. A ready-to-drink shake is an emulsified liquid — protein with oils, stabilizers, and flavors. Neither behaves like plain powder in preparation.

That finished-product character is the point. Ingredient data from USDA FoodData Central describes components in general; it cannot describe your bar after processing or your shake after homogenization. Tell us the full product as sold — flavor, coating, inclusions — because all of it is part of the sample.

What we measure in it

We measure amino acids using AQC (AccQ-Tag) pre-column derivatization with HPLC-UV.

For most bar and shake profile questions, total amino acid analysis is the relevant form: the protein is bound inside the product, and hydrolysis is needed to measure the full profile. Free amino acid analysis (AA-1) answers a narrower question — the amino acids already free in the product — which fits products with added free amino acids or a specific free-amino claim.

Within the panel, the essential amino acids, including the branched-chain amino acids, usually draw the most attention because they are the ones profile claims are built around. The full panel still matters. A shift that shows up across many analytes points to a different problem — a protein-source or dosing change — than a shift in a single analyte.

If you are not sure which form fits, describe the claim or specification at submission. Choosing free when you needed total is the most common way to waste a finished-product sample.

Panels and prices

Sample and submission

Send bars in their retail form, in packaging, labeled with product name, flavor, and lot. Send shakes as the sealed retail liquid where possible. Follow the amount guidance on our sample-submission page — if you are unsure how many units to send, email info@openchemworks.com first rather than guessing.

Mail-in submission is available. Because we are validating now and reserving pilot slots, contact us before shipping. Tell us whether the product is a bar, a ready-to-drink shake, or a dry mix, and what decision the result supports. Finished products vary a lot, and that context determines preparation.

What the report shows

The report shows per-analyte results, the reporting basis used, and the chromatogram behind each number. Read the basis carefully: results on the finished product are not directly comparable to results on the protein ingredient alone.

For run-to-run comparisons, keep product, flavor, panel, and basis identical — a coated bar and an uncoated bar are different samples. If you send a comparison set, say so at submission so reporting stays consistent.

FAQ

Should I test the powder or the finished bar?
If the decision is about the product you sell, test the finished product. Ingredient testing answers an ingredient question.

Do I want free or total analysis?
Usually total (AA-T) for profile verification of the bound protein. Free (AA-1) fits added free amino acids or a free-amino claim. Ask us with your claim in hand.

Does flavor or coating change the sample?
Yes — it is part of the finished product. Send and label the exact flavor and format you need a decision on.

Is cysteine included?
No. Cysteine is excluded from AA-1 and requires a separate AA-3 workup.

Are you accredited for this testing?
No. OpenChemWorks is not GLP, GMP, or ISO 17025 accredited.
