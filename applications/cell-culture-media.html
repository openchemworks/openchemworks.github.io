Amino Acids in Cell Culture Media

What amino acids do in a culture medium, which ones run low during a run, and how to measure them in fresh, fed and spent media.

Order samplesView Pricing
Short answer

Amino acids are the building blocks cells use to make their own protein and the product they are growing. They also feed the cell: glutamine in particular is a nitrogen and energy source. Essential amino acids cannot be made by the cells, so the medium has to supply them. Cells use each amino acid at a different rate, so some run low during a run while others stay high. Glutamine also breaks down to ammonia by itself, even with no cells present.

Which amino acids run low first

It depends on the medium and the cells, but the published picture is consistent on where to look first.

  • Glutamine. In CHO cultures glutamine often shows the highest demand of any amino acid, particularly during exponential growth (glutamine, glutamate and ammonia across a fed-batch run, citing Yatipanthalawa et al., Biotechnology Advances, 2026). It is also 26 to 38% of the starting amino acid pool in DMEM, DMEM/F-12, RPMI-1640 and Ham's F-12. Using one published CHO consumption rate (Avilan Garzon et al., Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology, 2025) as a constant-density model, glutamine runs out in about 1 to 4 days at 1×106 cells/mL, depending on the medium. That is an illustration, not a prediction for your culture.
  • Asparagine, where the medium has it. In the same model asparagine runs out faster than glutamine: 0.13 days in DMEM/F-12, 0.26 in Ham's F-12 and 0.98 in RPMI-1640. DMEM contains no asparagine to begin with.
  • Tryptophan moves first in percentage terms. It is the smallest molar pool in all four media, so even low consumption shifts it visibly.
  • Some start at zero. DMEM contains no alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, glutamic acid or proline, so anything you measure for those in spent DMEM was made by the cells.

Glutamine is also fragile. In solution it breaks down to pyroglutamate and ammonia with no cells present: in the classic measurement about 10% per day at 37 °C, against about nine days for the same loss at 4 °C (Tritsch and Moore, Experimental Cell Research, 1962). So falling glutamine with rising ammonia can be chemistry, cell metabolism or both. Read them together, and against a matched fresh sample.

Starting concentrations for four common media and the full depletion model are in the spent media depletion atlas. For media that has supported cells, see spent media analysis.

What we measure in fresh media

A fresh medium has a formulation to compare against, so the analysis is a verification. Concentration also changes behavior. A feed concentrate holds amino acids at many times basal levels, where some approach their solubility and some degrade in storage, even in the refrigerator.

Glutamine is the standard example. It breaks down to pyroglutamate and ammonia, faster at higher temperature, so a stored medium can carry less glutamine and more ammonia than it was made with. Falling glutamine with rising ammonia is the signature. We do not measure pyroglutamate itself. We track the glutamine loss and the ammonia rise, which is what the panel reports. Our guide to glutamine in cell culture media works through it.

Cysteine and cystine are different compounds. Cystine is two cysteines linked by a disulfide bond, and cysteine can oxidize to cystine over time. AA-1 does not include either, and AA-3 covers them. Asparagine can convert to aspartate on storage.

Caveats for culture media

Alanyl-glutamine is not free glutamine

Many media supply glutamine as the dipeptide alanyl-glutamine (GlutaMAX). In a 21-day CAR-T expansion it was entirely cleaved over the run (Jensen et al., Scientific Reports, 2025). AA-1 reports free glutamine and free alanine. The intact dipeptide is not counted as glutamine, so low free glutamine early in a dipeptide-fed culture does not by itself mean the glutamine source is used up.

Freeze promptly and keep tubes capped

Ammonia is volatile. In a CAR-T sampling study, samples kept in a refrigerator had significantly lower ammonia than samples frozen immediately, and the authors suggest ammonia evaporated during storage (Chan et al., Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology, 2025). Freeze samples promptly after collection, keep them capped, and store every sample in a time course the same way.

Glutamine breaks down without any cells

Glutamine in medium turns into ammonia on its own. In one study, pre-warmed or room-temperature medium produced over 20-fold more ammonium than fresh medium, while media without L-glutamine or with GlutaMAX stayed close to the detection limit (Kegel et al., Virology, 2025). Send a fresh sample handled the same way as your culture samples, so the chemical share of the ammonia can be separated from what the cells made.

Serum, platelet lysate and albumin

Serum, platelet lysate, albumin and cytokine carriers add protein, and protein carries amine groups that react with AQC. We remove protein before derivatization, and a PREP-2 spike-recovery check ($200 per assay, once per formulation, waived at 20+ samples) confirms recovery the first time we see a new medium. Cysteine and cystine are not in AA-1. AA-3 covers them, priced after a free call. Asparagine can convert to aspartate on storage, and the profile will show it.

FAQ

What is the role of amino acids in culture medium?
Amino acids are the building blocks cells use to make their own protein and the product they are growing. Glutamine also serves as a nitrogen and energy source. Essential amino acids cannot be made by the cells, so the medium must supply them. Cells use each one at a different rate, so some run low during a run while others stay high.

Which amino acids run out first in cell culture?
Glutamine is often the most heavily consumed amino acid in CHO runs, especially during exponential growth. In one published CHO consumption model, glutamine ran out in about 1 to 4 days at 1×10⁶ cells/mL depending on the medium, and asparagine, where a medium contains it, ran out faster still. Your own culture will differ, so measure it at each time point.

How do I measure amino acids in cell culture media?
Send clarified spent medium from each time point, frozen promptly and kept capped. AA-1 reports 19 free amino acids plus ammonia in each sample, using AQC derivatization with HPLC and dual UV and fluorescence detection. Every report includes the chromatogram, calibration and QC. Cysteine and cystine are not in AA-1.
