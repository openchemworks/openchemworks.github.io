## 1. Lead

Cell-free systems — lysate-based protein synthesis and related cell-free expression setups — run a biochemical reaction without living cells. Amino acids go in as substrates, protein comes out, and the supernatant left at the end records how the reaction actually behaved. Researchers and platform teams send cell-free supernatants for amino acid analysis when a reaction's yield dropped, when a lysate batch changes, or when a reaction recipe is being tuned and the question is which substrates ran short.

This is not a small version of cell culture: no cells over days, but a reaction consuming substrates over hours, in a dense extract matrix. For conventional culture, see the fresh and spent media hubs below.

What this sample type is

A cell-free supernatant is what remains after a cell-free reaction is stopped and solids are removed. It starts from a reaction mixture that typically includes a cell extract or lysate, an energy system, salts, buffers, and amino acids supplied as building blocks for the protein being made. The extract itself also contributes a background of amino acids and ammonia.

That background is the analytical catch. Unlike a defined medium, a lysate brings an amino acid signature of its own, which can differ between extract preparations. A supernatant profile read without a starting-mixture or extract-only comparison can mislead: a high value may be leftover substrate or lysate background, and the measurement alone cannot tell you which. Paired samples — reaction start and end, or extract blank alongside — turn a profile into an answer.

What we measure in it

Our free panel, AA-1, measures 19 amino acids plus ammonia by AQC (AccQ-Tag) pre-column derivatization with HPLC-UV. The free panel fits this sample type because cell-free reactions work from free amino acids as substrates; the question is normally the free pool before and after the reaction, not total protein composition.

Read results three ways. Depletion shows which substrates the reaction drew down hardest — candidates for why yield levelled off, and for recipe adjustment. Persistence shows what was supplied in excess of what the reaction used. Ammonia, reported as its own analyte, tracks a by-product that accumulates as amino acids are broken down in the extract, and forms part of the reaction's changing chemical environment.

We state the limit plainly: the panel profiles amino acids and ammonia. It does not measure your product protein, reaction energy substrates, or nucleotides, and a good amino acid profile alone does not explain every yield problem. Cysteine is excluded from AA-1 and requires a separate AA-3 workup.

What the report shows

The report lists each measured amino acid individually, plus ammonia, on a stated reporting basis, with the chromatogram behind each number available. For paired or blank-matched sets, results are presented sample by sample under your labels so start-to-end change is readable. Lysate supernatants are dense matrices, so spike recovery and matrix-effect qualification belong in the work, and the report states what was and was not qualified. We do not subtract a background you did not submit a blank for.

FAQ

How is this different from spent media?
Spent media comes from living cells over days in a defined medium. A cell-free supernatant comes from a lysate reaction over hours, with an extract background a defined medium does not have. Different question, different controls.

Do I really need a blank or starting sample?
For depletion claims, yes. Without one, the profile describes the end state only, and lysate background cannot be separated from leftover substrate.

Can the profile explain a yield drop?
It can show whether amino acid substrates ran short or accumulated oddly. It cannot rule on energy substrates, product folding, or other causes outside the panel.
