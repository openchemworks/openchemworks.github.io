Valine

Published October 29, 2026Updated November 19, 20264 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Valine is one of the standard amino acids that make up proteins, and it also occurs on its own, unattached, in foods, fermentation samples, and cell culture media. A lab reports valine as part of a wider amino acid profile, not in isolation. Valine is one of the three branched-chain amino acids, alongside leucine and isoleucine, and on a panel report each is reported as its own result, not as a combined branched-chain total.

Chemistry in plain terms

Standard biochemistry references classify valine as an essential amino acid for humans, meaning it must come from food because the body cannot make it. Its side chain is a branched hydrocarbon group, which is why valine, leucine, and isoleucine are grouped as the branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) in standard classifications. PubChem lists L-valine as a standard proteinogenic amino acid, built into protein chains as well as present free in solution. Both forms of presence, bound and free, are real, and they answer different questions.

Grouped for classification does not mean interchangeable: the three are different compounds, and a panel result keeps them separate.

Where it occurs and what drives the level

Valine occurs as a building block in proteins across foods, feeds, and biological materials. Free valine also occurs in cell culture and spent media, fermentation and process liquids, protein hydrolysates, and food and supplement products, where a free profile describes the sample as it is.

Free valine in a finished BCAA product is set by the formulation, so the result is checked against the label per serving. In foods and protein ingredients most valine is protein-bound and appears only in a total result. In media it is a supplied nutrient that cells draw down. Because label claims cover valine, leucine and isoleucine together, read the three from the same run. Valine, leucine and isoleucine are resolved as separate peaks.

See it in practice: BCAA testing for dietary supplements; BCAA testing for protein powders.

Free vs total valine

A free result measures valine already unattached in the sample as received, with no hydrolysis step. It answers questions about the product, medium or liquid as it is.

A total result is measured after acid hydrolysis (6 N HCl, 110 °C, 24 h), which releases valine bound in protein. Total includes the free fraction, so it is larger in protein-rich samples. By how much depends on the sample, so measure both when the question needs both.

What hydrolysis does to valine. Valine bonds (Val-Val, Val-Ile) hydrolyse slowly, so a 24 h hydrolysis can under-recover valine in a protein sample. Treat total valine as a minimum for such samples. Free valine is not affected.

How valine is measured here

Valine is measured in the AA-1 panel by AQC (AccQ-Tag) pre-column derivatization, then HPLC with dual UV (260 nm) and fluorescence detection. Both signals are used to confirm each peak. Performance figures come from the published method; every report includes our own calibration and QC for the run. Reports also show AABA, the internal standard we add to every sample. It is not a result for your sample. See how the method works.

Frequently asked questions

Is valine reported as part of a combined BCAA total?
No. Valine, leucine and isoleucine are grouped as branched-chain amino acids, and each is reported as its own result.

Does 24 h hydrolysis recover all the valine?
Not always. Valine bonds release slowly, so a total result can read low in a protein sample. For added free BCAAs, use the free panel.

Where do I check a BCAA label?
On the BCAA pages for supplements and protein powders, which cover per-serving math and the label ratio.
