Valine

Published October 29, 20264 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Valine is one of the standard amino acids that make up proteins, and it also occurs on its own, unattached, in foods, fermentation samples, and cell culture media. A lab reports valine as part of a wider amino acid profile, not in isolation. Valine is one of the three branched-chain amino acids, alongside leucine and isoleucine, and on a panel report each is reported as its own result, not as a combined branched-chain total. One method point also matters: AABA (alpha-aminobutyric acid) is the internal standard the lab adds to help quantify results. AABA is not valine and is not a result for your sample.

Chemistry in plain terms

Standard biochemistry references classify valine as an essential amino acid for humans, meaning it must come from food because the body cannot make it. Its side chain is a branched hydrocarbon group, which is why valine, leucine, and isoleucine are grouped as the branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) in standard classifications. PubChem lists L-valine as a standard proteinogenic amino acid, built into protein chains as well as present free in solution. Both forms of presence, bound and free, are real, and they answer different questions.

Grouped for classification does not mean interchangeable: the three are different compounds, and a panel result keeps them separate.

Where it occurs

Valine occurs as a building block in proteins across foods, feeds, and biological materials. Free valine also occurs in cell culture and spent media, fermentation and process liquids, protein hydrolysates, and food and supplement products, where a free profile describes the sample as it is.

This page gives no typical level for any material. Levels vary with recipe, organism, process, and sampling point, and no verified figure is available. Measure your sample rather than borrowing a number from a different one.

Free vs total for valine

A free valine result measures the valine already dissolved and unattached in the sample as received, with no hydrolysis step. That is the relevant number when the question is about the sample as used or as eaten: what is in the medium now, or what free profile a product presents.

A total valine result is measured after acid hydrolysis, the step that breaks protein chains apart and releases bound amino acids. A total result therefore includes free valine plus valine that was bound inside protein. This page gives no ratio between free and total for any material, because that ratio is a property of the specific sample.

Valine has no special hydrolysis conversion of the kind that affects asparagine and glutamine, whose amide groups are lost during acid hydrolysis and are reported together with aspartic acid and glutamic acid respectively. The total-panel caution applies: a hydrolysed measurement describes the sample after the method has acted on it, not a photograph of its native state.

Method notes: how valine is measured here

At OpenChemWorks, valine is measured as part of a panel using AQC pre-column derivatization, the Waters AccQ-Tag approach, with HPLC-UV detection. The amino acids are chemically tagged, separated on the HPLC column, and quantified against calibrated standards. For the branched-chain group, the method requirement is that valine, leucine, and isoleucine are separated on the column so each can be quantified separately. That is stated as a method requirement only: no resolution, recovery, or detection-limit figure is quoted, because no verified figure from this laboratory's validated method is available. AABA is added at a known amount as the internal standard, which is why its name appears in method paperwork. To restate the point: AABA is the standard, added by the lab; valine is the analyte, measured from your sample. Different compounds, different roles.

Which panel includes valine

Valine is included in AA-1, the free amino acid panel: 19 amino acids plus ammonia, measured by AQC HPLC-UV. Cysteine is excluded from AA-1 and needs a separate AA-3 workup. Valine is also part of a total result under AA-T, where the sample is hydrolysed first and valine is reported on that total basis.

Frequently asked questions

Is AABA the same as valine?
No. AABA, alpha-aminobutyric acid, is the internal standard added by the lab for quantification. Valine is a different compound, and it is the analyte measured from your sample.

Is valine reported as part of a combined BCAA total?
No. Valine, leucine, and isoleucine are grouped as branched-chain amino acids for classification, and each is reported as its own result in the panel.

Do I need free or total valine?
Free answers a question about the sample as received. Total answers a question about protein composition after hydrolysis, and includes the free fraction.

Is valine reported on its own?
At OpenChemWorks it is reported as part of the AA-1 free panel or the AA-T total panel, alongside the panel analytes, including leucine and isoleucine as separate results.
