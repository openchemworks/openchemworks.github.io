Glutamic acid

Published October 26, 20264 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Glutamic acid is the amino acid behind glutamate, the savoury, umami signal in foods, seasonings, and fermentation broths. It is reported either as free glutamic acid, already present as eaten or in solution, or as total glutamic acid after hydrolysis breaks proteins apart. Those numbers answer different questions. This page explains the difference, shows how a free result converts to a monosodium glutamate (MSG) equivalent on a stated basis using verified molar masses, and names which panel reports each version. Taste and formulation decisions are usually free questions. Protein composition may be a total question.

Chemistry in plain terms

Glutamic acid is an acidic amino acid. In foods and most near-neutral solutions it is present mainly in its ionised form, glutamate, which is why reports switch between the two words. They describe the same amino acid in different chemical states, not two ingredients. MSG is its sodium salt, supplied dry either without added water in its crystal structure (anhydrous) or with one water per unit (monohydrate). The salt weighs more per unit of glutamate than glutamic acid itself, so converting between them is molar-mass arithmetic, not guesswork.

Glutamic acid differs from glutamine by one amide group, a small difference that matters in total analysis, below. No health or safety claim is made here.

Where glutamic acid occurs

Free glutamate occurs naturally in savoury foods and ingredients including tomatoes, aged cheeses, fermented sauces, yeast extracts, and protein hydrolysates, and in seasoning blends where MSG or a glutamate-rich ingredient was added. Bound glutamic acid occurs in almost every protein, built into protein chains. Fermentation and cell culture media can also contain it, as a remainder of microbial or cellular activity.

A result measures the combined amount from all sources at once. It cannot tell you how much MSG powder was added, which ingredient contributed what, or whether a product meets a labelling rule.

Free vs total for glutamic acid

Free glutamic acid is measured without hydrolysis: the as-eaten question. Use it for savoury strength, comparing formulations as eaten, and an MSG equivalent for a serving as consumed.

Total glutamic acid is measured after acid hydrolysis breaks protein chains, so it is free plus formerly bound glutamic acid. It will be larger where a sample has substantial protein, by an amount depending on the sample. No free-to-total ratio is given for any food, because no general ratio is valid.

Hydrolysis is special here. During acid hydrolysis, glutamine converts to glutamic acid, qualitatively, and asparagine converts to aspartic acid. A total result therefore includes glutamate that was originally glutamine. It is a hydrolysed-sample measurement, not a photograph of native glutamic acid alone. That is the British Journal of Nutrition (2023) point, with no conversion percentage stated.

Reporting bases and the MSG conversion. Name the basis before comparing: glutamic acid, glutamate, MSG anhydrous equivalent, or MSG monohydrate equivalent. Verified PubChem molar masses: glutamic acid 147.13, MSG anhydrous 169.11, MSG monohydrate 187.13 (CIDs 33032 and 23672308). As MSG anhydrous equivalent, multiply a glutamic acid result by 169.11 / 147.13, about 1.149. As MSG monohydrate equivalent, multiply by 187.13 / 147.13, about 1.272. Worked shape only, no sample data: 100 units as glutamic acid are about 114.9 units as MSG anhydrous equivalent, or about 127.2 units as MSG monohydrate equivalent. State which form you used; mixing bases creates a false difference.

Method notes: AQC AccQ-Tag HPLC-UV

Free glutamic acid is measured inside AA-1 by AQC (AccQ-Tag) pre-column derivatization with HPLC-UV: tagged, separated by high-performance liquid chromatography, and measured by ultraviolet detection against calibrated standards. Total glutamic acid is measured after hydrolysis, then the same way. No performance number is stated: no detection limit, precision, or recovery figure. For salty, dark, or oily matrices, matrix-effect qualification, checking distortion with a known spike in your sample, may be needed and is priced on inquiry.

Frequently asked questions

Is glutamate the same as glutamic acid?
They describe the same amino acid in different chemical states. What matters is the stated reporting basis, especially for an MSG equivalent.

Which MSG form should I use?
The form matching the ingredient you actually use, stated on every comparison. Factors: about 1.149 for anhydrous, about 1.272 for monohydrate.

Will total glutamic acid tell me how savoury a food is?
No. Savoury impact as eaten follows the free fraction. Total includes bound glutamate not free to taste until digestion or hydrolysis.

Why is total higher than the recipe suggests?
Bound glutamic acid, plus glutamine converted during hydrolysis, both feed the total. That is expected method behaviour, not by itself evidence of added MSG.

Can a result prove how much MSG was added?
No. Glutamate has many sources. A result measures the combined amount on the stated basis.
