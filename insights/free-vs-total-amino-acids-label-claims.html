Free vs total amino acids: what a label claim actually needs, per matrix

Published October 30, 20266 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

A claim about amino acids that are already free in the product needs a free measurement; a claim about the amino acids bound in a protein needs a total measurement (acid hydrolysis first); a claim about how far a protein has been broken down needs both, because the answer is the difference between them.

The one-sentence rule

A claim about amino acids that are already free in the product needs a free measurement; a claim about the amino acids bound in a protein needs a total measurement (acid hydrolysis first); a claim about how far a protein has been broken down needs both, because the answer is the difference between them.

Ordering the wrong one does not give a weaker answer — it gives an answer to a different question. A free panel on a whey powder reads near zero for most amino acids, not because the powder is empty but because its amino acids are bound. A total panel on a drink spiked with free glycine counts that glycine as if it belonged to the protein.

Companion, not a duplicate: the methods page Free vs Total Amino Acids: Which You Need explains the two preparations in general. This note is the label-claim companion: it works claim by claim, matrix by matrix.

Three traps this prevents

1. The nitrogen shortcut. Grams of protein on a Nutrition Facts panel may be calculated from total nitrogen (nitrogen × 6.25, per 21 CFR 101.9). That is a nitrogen measurement, not an amino acid measurement: cheap free amino acids added to a powder raise the nitrogen number exactly as intact protein does. A per-amino-acid profile — free, total, or the pair — is what separates "protein by arithmetic" from protein by composition.

2. Reading a total number as a free claim (and the reverse). If a drink claims a specific amount of free leucine, a total result cannot support it: the total folds in any leucine still bound in peptides. If a powder claims a full essential-amino-acid composition, a free result cannot support it: most of those amino acids never appear in the free pool. Match the preparation to the claim's own words.

3. Forgetting what hydrolysis changes. A total result is not the free result "plus more of everything". Under the standard conditions (6 N HCl, 110 °C, 24 hours — live AA-T service page), tryptophan is destroyed and cysteine/cystine is not reported, so AA-T reports 16 amino acids; asparagine and glutamine are converted, so aspartate and glutamate are reported as the combined Asx and Glx; serine, threonine and methionine carry wider uncertainty from partial losses. A label claim that turns on tryptophan, or on glutamine as glutamine, cannot be settled by a total panel at all — it needs the free panel (where both are reported as themselves) or a separate workup. State this before ordering, not after the report lands.

Worked example (illustrative — not laboratory data)

A pea-protein drink mix claims "10 g protein per serving, complete amino acid profile".

  • Free panel ordered alone: most amino acids read at trace levels. Wrong conclusion available: "the product is empty". Correct reading: the test skipped the protein.
  • Total panel ordered: per-amino-acid composition in g/100 g, Asx/Glx combined, no tryptophan figure. This supports the composition claim for the 16 reported amino acids — and the report must flag that tryptophan, often the amino acid a "complete protein" claim turns on for plant proteins, is not among them.
  • What the brand actually needed: the total panel for composition, plus an explicit plan for the tryptophan gap, agreed before sampling — not a surprise in the delivered report.

What to send, and what the report must show

  • Name the claim first. Send the exact front-panel wording with the sample. The lab's job is to test that sentence, and the free/total choice follows from it.
  • Per-amino-acid numbers, not a single total. mg per serving (free claims) or g/100 g and mol% (total composition), each amino acid next to the label figure it is being checked against.
  • The preparation, stated. Free: protein removed, no hydrolysis. Total: 6 N HCl, 110 °C, 24 hours, with the hydrolysis artifacts above carried into the report's notes.
  • The chromatogram and the run's QC summary, with calibration against reference standards — the evidence behind the numbers, as on every OpenChemWorks report.

Honest limits

  • This note describes which measurement a claim needs. It is not a statement of validated capability: OpenChemWorks' AQC/HPLC method is in development, with validation toward Nov 20, 2026, and the laboratory is not GLP, GMP or ISO/IEC 17025 accredited. Results support product and label-claim decisions, not regulatory submission.
  • Amino acid analysis does not detect non-amino-acid ingredients (creatine, caffeine and sweeteners each need their own assay), and no amino acid panel by itself establishes a protein-quality score — digestibility is a separate input.
  • One sample is one lot at one moment. A label claim is a per-lot promise; the per-lot verification plan in the BCAA companion note applies here unchanged.
  • Taurine and the extended panel are on-request add-ons, not part of the standard panels named above.
