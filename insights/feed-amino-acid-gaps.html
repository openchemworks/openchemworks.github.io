Summary: key findings

All figures below are our computations from two published inputs: NRC requirement tables (swine NRC 2012 as republished by Merck Veterinary Manual; poultry NRC 1994 as republished by Merck Veterinary Manual and Purdue Extension AS-584-W) and published ingredient amino acid profiles (Evonik AMINODat-style feed table: corn and soybean meal, n = 765 and n = 773 samples respectively). Diet archetypes, formulas, and every intermediate number are in data.csv and calc-notes.md.

  • In a 78% corn / 20% soybean meal diet for 25–50 kg growing pigs, every key amino acid falls short, and lysine falls furthest. Computed diet lysine is 0.757% against a 1.12% requirement: 67.6% of requirement, a gap of 0.363 percentage points. Methionine + cystine reaches 82.0% of requirement, threonine 82.8%, methionine alone 80.3%. Limiting order: lysine first, methionine (as Met+Cys) second, threonine third.
  • Closing the pig lysine gap with synthetic lysine alone takes 0.47% L-lysine HCl. Worked: gap 0.363% ÷ 0.78 (lysine content of L-Lys HCl) = 0.465% of diet, or 4.65 kg per tonne, before the next-limiting amino acids are addressed. Threonine then needs a further 0.126% L-threonine (gap 0.124% ÷ 0.985).
  • The same two ingredients in broiler-starter proportions (60% corn / 35% soybean meal) flip the problem. Lysine (1.133% vs 1.10% required, 103.0%), threonine (101.6%), tryptophan (128.0%), and arginine (113.0%) all clear NRC 1994. Only the sulfur amino acids fail: methionine at 63.8% of requirement and Met+Cys at 73.9%. For broilers on corn-soy, methionine is first-limiting, exactly as the poultry literature states, and our ingredient arithmetic reproduces it from raw profiles.
  • The fix the arithmetic implies for broilers is 0.18–0.24% DL-methionine. Closing the methionine-only gap takes 0.181% ÷ 0.99 = 0.183% DL-Met; closing the full Met+Cys gap with methionine takes 0.235% ÷ 0.99 = 0.237%. Published commercial starter formulas typically include about 0.23–0.30% DL-methionine, which brackets our computed range.
  • Corn is the structural cause in both species. Corn carries 0.25% lysine and 0.35% Met+Cys at 8.4% crude protein; soybean meal carries 2.81% lysine and 1.30% Met+Cys at about 46% crude protein. Every 10 points of diet swapped from corn to soybean meal adds 0.256 points of lysine but only 0.095 points of Met+Cys. Lysine gaps close with ingredient swaps; sulfur gaps mostly do not. That asymmetry is why synthetic methionine is the first purchase in poultry and synthetic lysine is the first purchase in swine.

So what: the limiting-amino-acid order is not a matter of opinion or of which supplier's brochure you read. It falls out of two public tables and one weighted average. If your formulation software disagrees with the order above for these archetypes, the disagreement is in its ingredient matrix values, and an assay of your actual corn and soybean meal lots is how you settle it.

Why it matters: formulating to book values when your lots run below them is a silent performance tax: the pig diet above is short 32% of its lysine requirement before a single pig eats. Synthetic amino acids are cheap insurance only when they are aimed at the amino acid that is actually first-limiting.

Introduction

A corn and soybean meal diet can look adequate on crude protein and still fail the animal on individual amino acids. This article asks a narrow, checkable question: for two standard corn-soy diets, one for growing pigs and one for broiler starters, how much of each key amino acid do the ingredients actually deliver, compared with what the requirement tables say the animal needs?

We computed the answer from two published inputs only: NRC requirement tables, for swine as republished by the Merck Veterinary Manual and for poultry as republished by Merck and Purdue Extension, and published ingredient amino acid profiles for corn and soybean meal. Each diet is a weighted average of those two profiles, set against the requirement, amino acid by amino acid. From that one calculation come the supply ratios, the limiting order, and the amounts of synthetic amino acid that would close each gap.

Read the tables that follow as an audit trail: supply, requirement, ratio and gap are shown for every amino acid, with a worked example, so any formulator can reproduce the arithmetic or rerun it with different ingredient values. That is also the practical point for buyers. Book values are population averages, so where a ration underperforms, an assay of the actual corn and soybean meal lots is how a disagreement about the limiting order gets settled.

Worked example (lysine): 0.78 × 0.25 + 0.20 × 2.81 = 0.195 + 0.562 = 0.757%. Ratio: 0.757 / 1.12 = 0.676. Synthetic closure: 0.363 / 0.78 = 0.465% L-Lys HCl.

Diet crude protein computes to 15.75%, close to the protein level NRC associates with this phase when lysine is met from corn-soy, which is a useful sanity check on the archetype: the diet looks adequate on crude protein while failing on lysine. That is the whole point of amino-acid-based formulation.

Worked example (Met+Cys): 0.60 × 0.35 + 0.35 × 1.30 = 0.210 + 0.455 = 0.665%. Ratio: 0.665 / 0.90 = 0.739. Synthetic closure: 0.235 / 0.99 = 0.237% DL-methionine (99%) if the entire sulfur gap is closed with methionine, which is legitimate because methionine converts to cysteine in the bird and pig; the reverse is not true.

Perspectives

Formulator. Ideal-protein ratios read straight off Table 1: at the point lysine is set to 100, the pig archetype delivers Met+Cys at 70.4% of lysine (0.533/0.757) against a requirement ratio of 58.0% (0.65/1.12), and threonine at 78.7% against a required 64.3%. Read carefully: once lysine is supplemented to requirement without adding protein, those ratios are what the supplemented diet must preserve, which is why Thr and Met+Cys become the binding constraints immediately after lysine. Supplement order for the pig archetype: Lys, then Thr/Met+Cys together. For the broiler archetype the order is Met first, and nothing else until sulfur is fixed.

Buyer of ingredients. Which purchases close a gap? Per tonne of diet, replacing 10 points of corn with soybean meal buys +2.56 kg lysine, +0.95 kg Met+Cys, +1.51 kg threonine. Replacing the same 10 points with synthetic product buys exactly the amino acid on the label and nothing else. When the gap is lysine-only ( pigs after Thr/Met are covered), synthetics win on precision; when several amino acids are short at once (the unsupplemented pig archetype), ingredient swaps close three gaps at once but drag crude protein and cost up with them.

Analytical. Total vs digestible basis changes the answer, and here is a worked conversion using published coefficients: Graham et al. 2013 (Kansas State) reported standardized ileal digestibility (SID) for fermented soybean meal in swine of 71.1% for lysine, 89.2% for methionine, 88.0% for threonine, 93.7% for tryptophan (AID values were lower: Lys 63.5%). Apply the lysine SID to a hypothetical diet carrying 1.12% total lysine from such an ingredient and the digestible supply is 1.12 × 0.711 = 0.796% SID lysine. Two lessons: (1) a diet that "meets requirement" on a total basis may not on an SID basis, because NRC 2012 swine requirements are properly expressed as SID; our Table 1 uses the total-basis requirement table republished by Merck for transparency, and the true SID gap for the pig archetype is larger, not smaller. (2) Digestibility is amino-acid-specific (Lys 71.1% vs Trp 93.7% in the same ingredient), so a single "protein digestibility" number cannot convert a profile. Caveat stated plainly: those coefficients are for fermented soybean meal, whose lysine SID ran below conventional soybean meal in that study; do not apply them to conventional meal. We use them to show the size and direction of the total-to-SID correction, not to re-rate the archetypes. Conventional corn and soybean meal SID coefficients from the INRAE/CIRAD/AFZ tables are the next data addition for this article (see gap note).

Cost. We do not publish synthetic amino acid prices in this version: we did not verify a current public price series for L-Lys HCl, DL-Met, and L-Thr on the access date, and §3 of this program forbids filling the hole with a remembered price. The quantities, however, are computed and stable: per tonne of pig feed, 4.65 kg L-Lys HCl plus 1.26 kg L-Thr plus about 1.18 kg DL-Met (Met+Cys gap 0.117 ÷ 0.99) closes this archetype; per tonne of broiler starter, 2.37 kg DL-Met closes the sulfur gap. Multiply by your contracted prices; the shopping list does not change.

What to do with this

Book values are population means (our corn lysine rests on 765 samples, soybean meal on 773). Your lots are draws from those distributions. If a phase is underperforming, assay the ingredients before reformulating on faith.

Check your own ingredients: Total amino acid analysis, $230/sample. An AA-T total panel on your corn, soybean meal, and finished feed replaces three book values with your values, and the gap tables above recompute in minutes.

Methods & Sources

Archetypes are defined by us and stated in full: swine 78% corn / 20% soybean meal / 2% amino-acid-free remainder; broiler 60% / 35% / 5%. Requirements: swine from the NRC 2012 table republished by Merck Veterinary Manual (total basis: Lys 1.12, Met 0.32, Met+Cys 0.65, Thr 0.72 for 25–50 kg, read from the 25–50 kg column of the growing-pig table); poultry from NRC 1994 via Merck Veterinary Manual broiler table and Purdue Extension AS-584-W (0–3 weeks: Lys 1.10, Met 0.50, Met+Cys 0.90, Thr 0.80, Trp 0.20, Arg 1.25). Ingredient profiles: AMINODat-style published feed table (corn n=765; soybean meal n=773) as reproduced in the AminoDat Aq compilation. Synthetic product purities used in closure math: L-Lys HCl 78% lysine, DL-Met 99%, L-Thr 98.5%: standard commercial specifications, stated as assumptions where used. Limitations: (1) total-basis comparison for swine understates the true SID gap (see Analytical perspective); (2) NRC 1994 poultry values predate modern broiler genetics; breeder-company specifications run higher, so Table 2 gaps are minimum gaps; (3) soybean meal crude protein in the source table is truncated in extraction at "46" (46% class meal assumed and flagged in data.csv); (4) swine tryptophan requirement was not extracted from a verified table in this pass, so swine Trp is computed for supply only (0.173%) and not ranked. That omission is a gap, not an oversight we paper over.

Sources (accessed November 5, 2026): Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutritional Requirements of Pigs and growing-pig requirements table; Merck Veterinary Manual, Nutrient Requirements of Broilers (NRC 1994); Purdue Extension AS-584-W, Protein and Amino Acid Requirements for Poultry; Graham et al. 2013, Kansas Agricultural Experiment Station Research Reports (via Feedipedia record); AminoDat Aq feed table compilation (Evonik data as reproduced). URLs in calc-notes.md.
