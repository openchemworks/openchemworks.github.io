Reading an amino acid lab report: µM to mg/L to mg/g, dilution and reporting bases

Published October 16, 2026Updated November 19, 20267 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

To read a lab report, find the basis first. Our AA-1 report prints each amino acid in µM and in mg/L. To get mg/L from µM, multiply by the molar mass and divide by 1,000. To get mg/g of your sample, multiply the mg/L in the sample solution by the dilution factor and the volume in litres, then divide by the sample mass in grams. The same glutamate result can be printed per gram, per 100 grams or per serving, and every one looks like a different number for identical chemistry.

Open an example report to follow along.

The 30-second method

Find five things, in this order. One: the analyte, for example glutamate reported as glutamic acid. Two: free or total, meaning whether the amino acids were measured as they occur in the food or after hydrolysis broke the protein apart. Three: the basis, the unit and denominator, such as µM, mg/L or mg/g. Four: the form of any conversion, for MSG equivalents anhydrous or monohydrate. Five: the evidence behind the number, the chromatogram, the calibration and QC results, and whether a matrix check was run on your sample. If any of the five is missing, ask for it before you compare the result to anything.

From µM to mg/L to mg/g

Three steps take you from the instrument number to a number about your sample.

  1. µM to mg/L. mg/L = µM x molar mass (g/mol) / 1,000. This gives the concentration in the solution that was injected, or in your liquid sample if it was reported undiluted.
  2. Apply the dilution factor. Concentration in the original liquid or extract = mg/L measured x dilution factor. If the lab diluted your sample 1 to 10 before derivatization, the dilution factor is 10. The report states the factor.
  3. mg/L to mg/g for a solid. mg/g = mg/L in the extract x extract volume (L) / sample mass (g). For a liquid sample, mg/L is already the answer; divide by the density in g/mL x 1,000 only if you need mg/g.

Worked example, illustrative and not laboratory data. A report shows 250 µM glutamic acid in a powder extract diluted 1 to 10. Step 1: 250 x 147.13 / 1,000 = 36.8 mg/L. Step 2: 36.8 x 10 = 368 mg/L in the extract. Step 3: if 1.00 g of powder was extracted into 20 mL (0.020 L), then 368 x 0.020 / 1.00 = 7.36 mg/g, which is 0.736 g per 100 g, as free glutamic acid.

Bases: the denominator is the meaning

A reporting basis states how much sample the amount refers to. Milligrams per gram (mg/g) says: in each gram of your sample, this many milligrams of the analyte were found. Milligrams per 100 grams (mg/100 g) is the same measurement scaled by 100, so 2 mg/g is 200 mg/100 g. Neither is more correct; food labels often favour per 100 g, while formulation work often favours per gram. Milligrams per serving adds a further step that depends entirely on the serving size someone chose, so a per-serving figure can look small or large without the chemistry changing. Convert both reports to the same basis before you compare them. If a report gives only per-serving numbers without the serving weight, the conversion back is impossible, so ask for the underlying basis.

Dry and wet bases add one more trap for moist foods. A result per gram of sample as received includes the water in that gram; a result per gram of dry matter removes it. For a dry seasoning the difference is small. For a sauce it can be substantial. A careful report states which it used.

The MSG equivalent, done carefully

Glutamate is often reported as glutamic acid, but recipes and labels talk about MSG, monosodium glutamate. Converting between them is a molar-mass ratio. The cited masses are glutamic acid, 147.13 g/mol; anhydrous MSG, 169.11 g/mol; and MSG monohydrate, the common commercial form, 187.13 g/mol. To express glutamic acid as anhydrous MSG equivalent, multiply by 169.11 / 147.13, about 1.149. To express it as MSG monohydrate equivalent, multiply by 187.13 / 147.13, about 1.272. The two equivalents differ by roughly a tenth, which is why a report must state its form.

Worked example, illustrative. A report states free glutamate as 10.0 mg/g, expressed as glutamic acid. As anhydrous MSG equivalent that is 10.0 x 1.149 = 11.5 mg/g. As MSG monohydrate equivalent it is 10.0 x 1.272 = 12.7 mg/g. Same sample, same measurement, three defensible numbers. None of them is the "real MSG you added": the measurement covers free glutamate from all sources in the sample. The conversion does not turn a free glutamate result into a total glutamate result. Total glutamate (Glx) comes from AA-T, after hydrolysis in 6 N hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 hours, and it includes glutamine converted to glutamate.

Frequently asked questions

My two reports disagree. Is one wrong?
Not necessarily. Put both on the same basis and the same form first. Only compare after that conversion.

How do I convert µM to mg/L?
Multiply the µM value by the molar mass in g/mol and divide by 1,000. For example, 250 µM glutamate is 250 x 147.13 / 1,000 = 36.8 mg/L.

Does mg/g mean the same as percent?
No. Percent is grams per 100 grams. A result of 10 mg/g is 1 g per 100 g, which is 1%. Converting explicitly prevents a tenfold misreading.

Can I convert a per-serving label figure back to mg/g?
Only if you know the exact serving weight in grams. Ask for it.

Which MSG form should I ask for?
Ask for the report to state glutamic acid as the primary result and name the MSG form used for any equivalent. Monohydrate is the common commercial form, but the choice must be explicit, not assumed.
