At a glance: what the numbers say

  • The free share of glutamate spans a 279-fold range across ordinary foods. Computed from USDA FoodData Central total glutamate and published free-glutamate measurements: free glutamate is 78.9% of total glutamate in soy sauce, 41.1% in dried shiitake, 32.5% in ripe tomato, 27.0% in green peas, 20.4% in sweet corn, 14.6% in Parmesan, 3.2% in cheddar, and 0.3% in milk. The total on a database line tells you almost nothing about the free number a label decision turns on.
  • Parmesan's reputation is a free-glutamate story, not a total-glutamate story. Parmesan holds 8,209 mg of total glutamate per 100 g (FoodData Central), of which 1,200 mg is free (14.6%). Cheddar holds 5,675 mg total, nearly 70% of Parmesan's total, but only 182 mg free. Parmesan delivers 6.6 times the free glutamate of cheddar on 1.45 times the total.
  • Milk is the control case. Whole milk carries 708 mg of total glutamate per 100 g and about 2 mg free: 99.7% of its glutamate is bound in protein and tastes of nothing until digestion. Any argument that treats "contains glutamate" as one fact fails on this row alone.
  • Soy sauce is the opposite control. At 1,246 mg free out of 1,579 mg total per 100 g, fermentation has already done the hydrolysis. Its free glutamate converts to 1,585 mg of MSG monohydrate equivalent per 100 g (1,432 mg on the anhydrous MSG basis), computed below.
  • The reporting basis changes the headline by 27%. The same free glutamate reads as glutamic acid (×1.000), anhydrous MSG (×1.149), or MSG monohydrate (×1.272). A specification that does not name its basis is ambiguous by more than a quarter before any laboratory touches the sample.
  • So what: If your decision is "no added MSG," an umami claim, or a seasoning specification, the number you need is free glutamate on a named basis. A total-amino-acid report, however accurate, cannot answer it, because 85 to 99.7% of what it counts in cheese and milk is bound.
  • Why it matters: Brands substantiate umami and MSG-related claims with whichever glutamate number is closest to hand, usually the total. The ledger below shows the error that introduces, food by food, and it is largest exactly where the claims are loudest: aged, fermented, and dried ingredients.

Introduction

How much of the glutamate in a food is actually free, and how much is bound in protein where it tastes of nothing until digestion? The two numbers answer different questions, yet brands, formulators, and specifications routinely reach for whichever one is closest to hand, usually the total. This article builds a ledger that keeps them apart, food by food.

The ledger is computed from two named sources: total glutamate from USDA FoodData Central, and free glutamate from published free-amino-acid food composition measurements. Bound glutamate is the total minus the free figure, and the free share is the free figure divided by the total, so every row can be re-performed from the table itself.

Read the first table for the ledger itself, in milligrams per 100 g, and the second for what happens when the same free figure is reported on different MSG bases. If a claim, an umami argument, or a seasoning specification is the decision at stake, the free number on a named basis is the one that matters, and the perspectives that follow show why substituting the total changes the answer.

Worked example (Table 1, Parmesan). Total: FoodData Central FDC 170848, Cheese, parmesan, hard: glutamic acid 8.209 g/100 g = 8,209 mg/100 g. Bound = 8,209 − 1,200 = 7,009 mg/100 g. Free % = 1,200 ÷ 8,209 × 100 = 14.6%. Parmesan vs cheddar free ratio = 1,200 ÷ 182 = 6.59 ≈ 6.6×; total ratio = 8,209 ÷ 5,675 = 1.45×.

Perspectives

Brand: "no added MSG" read against the numbers. The claim is about addition, and the ledger does not contradict it: a Parmesan-containing product can honestly say no MSG was added while carrying 1,526 mg of MSG monohydrate equivalent per 100 g of its Parmesan ingredient, naturally present and chemically identical once dissociated. What the ledger does contradict is any implication that the product is low in glutamate. Substantiate the narrow claim; do not let it imply the broad one.

Formulator: free glutamate per 100 g is the purchasing spec. Ranked on delivery: soy sauce 1,246, Parmesan 1,200, dried shiitake 1,060 mg free glutamate per 100 g, then a steep drop to peas at 200. Gap, reported as a finding: we did not publish a cost-per-gram ranking in this release because we could not verify comparable ingredient prices for all eight foods from named sources on the access date. The delivery ranking stands; the price ranking waits for verifiable prices rather than invented ones.

Analytical: why a total report cannot answer a free question. Total amino acid analysis hydrolyzes the sample first, which converts bound glutamate (and glutamine) into the measured pool by design. The free fraction, 0.3% to 78.9% of the total depending on the food, is destroyed as information by that step. A free-glutamate question requires a free amino acid analysis: extraction without hydrolysis. They are different measurements, priced and reported differently, and substituting one for the other is the single most common glutamate error we see.

Skeptic: natural vs added is a labeling distinction, not a chemical one. In solution, glutamate from Parmesan, from tomato, and from a bag of MSG is the same anion; taste receptors and metabolism do not carry a provenance field. The data show the quantities differ by food and process (aging, fermenting, drying all raise the free share), never the identity. Arguments on either side that start from the molecule are settled; arguments about labeling, dose, and matrix are the live ones.

Methods & Sources

  • Total glutamate (accessed November 5, 2026): USDA FoodData Central, SR Legacy, nutrient "Glutamic acid" (g/100 g × 1,000), retrieved per FDC ID from the FDC API: Parmesan hard 170848 (8.209), cheddar sharp sliced 170899 (5.675), tomato 170457 (0.431), soy sauce shoyu 174277 (1.579), green peas 170419 (0.741), sweet corn 169998 (0.636), whole milk 171265 (0.708), dried shiitake 168436 (2.579). Note: FDC total glutamic acid from acid hydrolysis includes glutamine converted to glutamate (Glx); disclosed here because it inflates "total glutamate" relative to a glutamate-only definition in every row, in the same direction.
  • Free glutamate (accessed November 5, 2026): Parmesan 1,200, tomato 140, peas 200, corn 130, milk (cow's) 2 mg/100 g: Giacometti (1979) free and bound glutamate compilation as reproduced in Loliger (2000) and the safefood MSG report table; cheddar (aged) 182 and dried shiitake 1,060 mg/100 g: same Giacometti lineage via the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics EAL umami white paper table; soy sauce 1,246 mg/100 g: Ninomiya (1998), Natural occurrence, Food Reviews International, as tabulated in secondary sources (Korean soy sauce). Full citations and URLs in calc-notes.md.
  • Molecular weights: glutamic acid 147.13, MSG anhydrous 169.11, MSG monohydrate 187.13 g/mol (standard published values for CAS 142-47-2 and its monohydrate).
  • Formulas: bound = total − free; free % = 100 × free/total; MSG equivalent = free glutamate × (MW_MSG basis ÷ 147.13).
  • Limitations: free and total values come from different samples, producers, and decades; the ratio is therefore a ledger estimate for a food class, not a measurement of one specimen. Parmesan free glutamate is published across a 1,200 to 1,680 mg/100 g range (ripening time is the main driver); we use 1,200, the Giacometti point value, and the ratio would be 20.5% at the top of the range. Tomato free glutamate rises with ripeness (published ripe values 140 to 250 mg/100 g); we use 140. A specimen-specific ratio requires measuring your specimen, which is the service this article feeds.
