Short answer

Normalizing spot urine amino acids solely by urinary creatinine systematically depresses amino acid ratios whenever study groups differ in skeletal muscle mass, age, sex, or exogenous creatine supplementation. On the bench, concentrated high-salt or high-urea urine can also drop reaction pH below the 8.2 to 10.1 window required for complete AQC derivatization unless pre-diluted and generously buffered.

This guide summarizes published urinary metabolomics normalization literature and AQC pre-column derivatization chemistry for research cohorts. It does not offer diagnostic reference intervals.

1. Why urinary creatinine normalization flips cohort trends

Spot urine concentrations fluctuate dramatically with hydration status, so researchers routinely divide every analyte by urinary creatinine (umol/mmol creatinine). However, creatinine is produced by non-enzymatic muscle creatine breakdown and scales directly with skeletal muscle mass, sex, age, and dietary creatine intake rather than kidney water excretion alone.

In nutrition, sports physiology, or cachexia research cohorts, subjects taking exogenous creatine or carrying higher lean mass excrete more baseline creatinine. Dividing their raw urinary amino acids by that elevated denominator systematically under-normalizes every amino acid, manufacturing false decreases across PCA/OPLS-DA models that disappear when normalized by osmolality or specific gravity (SG). Comparative work by Warrack et al. (2009) and Mervant, Tremblay-Franco et al. (2021) demonstrated that osmolality-based normalization avoids muscle-mass bias and improves multivariate group discrimination compared with creatinine alone.

3. High-salt & high-urea limits during AQC borate buffering

Unlike dilute culture media, concentrated urine carries heavy urea, phosphate, organic-acid, and sodium/potassium salt loads. Pre-column AQC derivatization (6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate) requires unprotonated amines and only proceeds quantitatively between pH 8.2 and 10.1 (typically targeted at pH 8.5 to 8.8).

  • Prevent buffer collapse: Concentrated or acidic urine can overwhelm tight sample-to-buffer ratios, depressing peak areas unevenly for basic or early-eluting amino acids. Keeping a robust borate buffer excess (7:1 to 10:1 v/v buffer-to-sample ratio) and pre-diluting concentrated urine (typically 1:5 to 1:20 into linear dynamic range) maintains target pH.
  • Monitor internal-standard recovery on every vial: Tracking an internal standard through derivatization flags high-salt suppression or reaction pH drift before data enters cohort statistics.
  • Avoid hydrolysis-incompatible preservatives: Freeze clarified urine promptly at -80 C to curb bacterial amino acid catabolism and ammonia rise; if submitting samples later for total hydrolyzed amino acids, avoid sodium azide preservatives that react under hot acid.
