How long do amino acids last in drinks? What is published, and how to measure yours

Short answer

BCAAs: no published decay rate exists for them in drinks, so the only honest answer is to measure your own product. Run AA-1 ($150 per sample) at bottling and at intervals and plot the slope. Two compounds do have published rates: glutamine loses about 0.23% a day in water at room temperature (43% left after a year), and aspartame loses more than a third of its content in two months at 30 °C in a citrus soda at pH 2.75.

BCAAs: no published rate, here is how to measure yours

The number on your label was true on the day of manufacture. At month nine the bottle is a different product, and without testing you are guessing which one the customer got. We searched for leucine, isoleucine and valine degradation rates in drinks and found no peer-reviewed rate constants to extract. BCAA loss in real drinks is more likely driven by interactions, such as Maillard chemistry with reducing sugars under heat, than by the clean first-order decay that glutamine and aspartame show. Anyone quoting you a BCAA half-life in a beverage is estimating.

A practical plan: test at day 0, 30, 90 and the end of the claimed shelf life, at the real storage temperature, plus one accelerated arm that you treat as a screen, not a result. Send 200 µL per sample (400 µL preferred). The AA-1 panel returns 19 amino acids plus ammonia in one run, so leucine, isoleucine and valine come with glutamine and the ammonia it releases. Four timepoints on one lot is 4 x $150 = $600. Free amino acids are the right measurement for a drink, because the amino acids are already free in solution and hydrolysis would only create artifacts.

The cleanest published numbers: aspartame in soft drinks

Researchers stored orange soft drinks sweetened with aspartame at three pH values and three temperatures for five months, measuring by HPLC (Yakici and Arici, 2013). After two months at 30 °C, 60.6% of the aspartame remained at pH 2.75, the acidity of a typical citrus soda, versus 79.2% at pH 3.25 and 89.7% at pH 4.57. After five months at 20 °C: 65.4%, 82.1% and 87.8%. Lower pH and higher temperature destroy more, and the effects compound. Aspartame is not a BCAA, but the lesson carries: acid, heat and time act on every label claim.

In flavoured dairy beverages at pH 6.4 to 6.7, measured aspartame half-lives were 24 to 58 days at 4 °C and 1 to 4 days at 30 °C, and dropping the pH from 6.7 to 6.4 doubled stability (Tsoubeli et al., 1994, from the published abstract). At pH 6.4 that is a 58 / 4 = 14.5-fold rate rise from 4 °C to 30 °C, a Q10 of 2.8. A European study of diet soft drinks found aspartame considerably decreased when samples were warmed to around 37 °C while its breakdown products rose (van Vliet et al., 2020). Glutamine becomes ammonia

Glutamine is the fastest mover among the free amino acids with published rates. It cyclizes to pyroglutamic acid and releases ammonia in equimolar amount, so if glutamine is on your label your stability question is really two questions: how much glutamine is left, and how much ammonia appeared. The rates below come from intravenous and cell culture solutions (Khan and Elia, 1991, and related work), not from commercial drinks. They are scenario bounds, not measurements of any drink. A drink is acidic, carbonated and flavoured, and glutamine's maximum stability is reported near pH 5 to 7.5, so a real drink could run faster or slower.

First-order arithmetic: fraction remaining = (1 minus r) to the power t, with r the daily fractional loss. Worked example, water row: 0.9977 to the power 365 = 0.432, so 43.2% remains after a year. Temperature is the biggest lever: the 37 °C rate is about 6 times the room-temperature rate in the same solution, matching the Arrhenius prediction from a published activation energy (98.7 kJ/mol, Wang et al., 1999). Intravenous solutions at 4 °C were reported at under 0.2% per day.

What a 12-month label claim costs in overage

To guarantee label amount L at day 365 you must fill L divided by the fraction remaining. At the plain-water glutamine rate that is L / 0.432 = 2.31 x L, a 131% overage. At the mixed-solution rate it is L / 0.053 = 18.9 x L, which is not a formulation plan. It shows that the formulation, not the overage, has to change. The options are a shorter claim, a colder chain, a pH move or a stabilised form.

A stable form: alanyl-glutamine

The glutamine dipeptide L-alanyl-L-glutamine has a published predicted shelf life (time to 90% remaining) of 5.3 years at 25 °C and pH 6.0 (European Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences, 1998). That is a daily loss of 0.0054%, about 43 times slower than free glutamine in water, and 98.0% remaining after one year. If free glutamine is on your label, the form matters more than the overage.

What this means for your label

The three levers are pH, temperature and time. Degradation is a rate, not an event, so "how long do amino acids last" has no single answer, only a curve. Measure the curve for your product, in your packaging. A release-day result tells you what you filled, not what the customer drinks at month nine. For a protein drink, see protein drink verification; for a supplement label check, see how to verify amino acids on a supplement label. Test the fastest mover: in a glutamine drink, glutamine and ammonia are the stability-indicating signals.

Frequently asked questions

Do BCAAs degrade in water?
Leucine, isoleucine and valine have simple aliphatic side chains, so they avoid the specific degradation pathways that hit cysteine, glutamine or tryptophan. In heat-processed drinks with reducing sugars they can still take part in Maillard chemistry. We found no published BCAA decay rate in a drink, so your product needs its own measurement.

Does refrigeration stop it?
It slows it. Temperature is the dominant lever in every stability study here, but slower is still a slope, and retail shelves are not refrigerated.

What about dry powder?
Most of this degradation needs water as the medium. The moment the powder becomes a drink, the clock starts. Test the drink.

Can one test tell me my shelf life?
No. One test is a snapshot. Two or more points over time give you the rate, and the rate lets you project.

Should a drink be tested for free or total amino acids?
Free. Hydrolysis exists to break protein apart, and the amino acids in an amino acid drink are already free. Acid hydrolysis would destroy tryptophan and convert glutamine and asparagine into other compounds.
