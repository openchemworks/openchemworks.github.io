Summary: key findings

  • Potato variety alone swings free asparagine 15.3-fold. Across 20 US retail potato varieties (Vivanti et al. 2006 data, republished in Finotti et al. 2011, Table 1), free asparagine ranged from 49.63 mg/100 g fresh weight (Purple, Peruvian) to 760.98 mg/100 g (Russet 2). 760.98 / 49.63 = 15.33. The median variety sits at about 117 mg/100 g, so the worst Russet lot carries roughly 6.5 times the median precursor load before any processing choice is made.
  • Per serving, the gap is over a gram. At a 150 g serving, free asparagine runs from 74.4 mg (Purple, Peruvian) to 1,141.5 mg (Russet 2): 49.63 × 1.5 = 74.4; 760.98 × 1.5 = 1,141.5.
  • Sugar does not explain the ranking. The highest-sugar variety (Yukon Gold grade B, 824.35 mg/100 g total free sugar) is third-lowest in asparagine (80.92 mg/100 g), and the highest-asparagine Russet 2 is mid-pack on sugar (374.33 mg/100 g). Asparagine-to-sugar ratios span 0.067 to 4.393, a 65-fold spread. In potato systems, variety selection on asparagine is the lever; sugar is a separate variable.
  • Cereals sit lower per 100 g, but vary almost as much within type. Wheat flour free asparagine spans 7.4 to 66.4 mg/100 g (Hamlet et al. range 74–664 mg/kg, reported in Processes 2023), a 9.0-fold spread inside one ingredient category. Rye flour spans 31.9 to 79.1 mg/100 g. Green Arabica coffee averages 48.6 mg/100 g (486 ± 97 µg/g, Li et al. 2023).
  • Benchmark levels are for finished food, not raw material. EU Regulation 2017/2158 sets acrylamide benchmarks of 500 µg/kg for ready-to-eat French fries, 750 µg/kg for potato crisps, 350 µg/kg for biscuits and wafers, and 400 µg/kg for roast coffee. No regulator publishes the precursor-side table a formulator starts from. This article is that table.

So what: If you buy potatoes, flour, or coffee by specification and the specification does not include free asparagine, you are not controlling your largest acrylamide variable. Variety and lot differences documented here are larger than most processing mitigations deliver.

Why it matters: Acrylamide mitigation programs usually start at the fryer or oven. The arithmetic above says the decision that matters most was made earlier, at ingredient selection, and it is measurable for the price of one free-amino-acid test per lot or variety.

Introduction

Acrylamide mitigation usually starts at the fryer or the oven, but the precursor load is decided much earlier, when a variety, a lot or a flour is chosen. This article asks the formulator’s starting question: how much free asparagine, the amino acid precursor that acrylamide forms from, does the raw material itself bring into the process, before any processing choice is made?

The atlas is built only from published free asparagine measurements. Its core is a ranked table of retail potato varieties, measured in the work of Vivanti and colleagues and republished by Finotti and colleagues, with each variety’s value also expressed per serving and in molar terms. Around it sit literature ranges for wheat and rye flour and a published mean for green coffee, presented as archetypes from different studies rather than as head-to-head measurements. No value here was estimated or interpolated.

Read the ranking as a specification tool, not as a prediction of a finished food’s acrylamide level, which also depends on sugar, moisture and process. What the table does show is where the starting risk sits and how large the ingredient lever is compared with later mitigations. It also shows its own limit: the same variety name, bought as different lots, can differ substantially, which is why variety selection is a strong signal and lot-level measurement is what closes the gap.

Worked example: unit conversion, wheat flour high end. 664 mg/kg ÷ 10 = 66.4 mg/100 g. All conversions in this article are mg/kg ÷ 10 = mg/100 g, or µg/g = mg/kg numerically.

Sulfur nutrition is a documented driver in wheat: the Polish study and the literature it reviews report grain asparagine of 3,435 mg/kg under sulfur deficiency versus 211 mg/kg under sulfur sufficiency (Curtis & Halford, reported in Agriculture 2024). That is a field-level variable your flour supplier's agronomy controls, and another reason a single book value for "wheat flour" is not a specification.

Perspectives

Formulator. Variety and lot selection is the first mitigation lever, and it is quantified above: switching from a Russet-type lot at 335–761 mg/100 g to a low-asparagine variety under 110 mg/100 g removes roughly two-thirds to nine-tenths of the precursor before blanching, asparaginase, or process changes are considered.

QA. The cheapest point to learn a lot's precursor load is at intake, on the raw material, not on finished-product acrylamide after the lot is committed. One free-amino-acid panel per variety or supplier lot answers it.

Regulatory. Benchmarks (EU 2017/2158: fries 500, crisps 750, biscuits/wafers 350, roast coffee 400 µg/kg) apply to finished food. Precursor math does not predict a finished acrylamide number on its own, because conversion depends on process, moisture, and sugar. It does rank your starting risk, which is what mitigation plans are supposed to do.

Analytical. Only the free number matters here. Total asparagine (from a hydrolysed, total-amino-acid run) includes protein-bound asparagine that is not available as acrylamide precursor in the same way, and hydrolysis converts asparagine to aspartate in any case (see Topic 15). This is a free-amino-acid measurement, not a total.

Methods & Sources

  • Inclusion: published free-asparagine measurements only. Total (hydrolysed) asparagine values were excluded, because they answer a different question.
  • Potato dataset: Finotti et al. (2011) Table 1, data originally in Vivanti et al. (2006), Journal of Food Science, retail varieties from Italy and the US. Sugar column is total free sugar (glucose + fructose + sucrose) as published; sucrose is not a reducing sugar, so the Asn:sugar ratio is a descriptive ratio, not a stoichiometric one. That limitation is stated wherever the ratio is used.
  • Flour and coffee values are literature ranges and means as cited in the table; they come from different studies, methods, and years, and are presented as archetype ranges, not head-to-head measurements.
  • No value in this article was estimated or interpolated. Where a study reported a range, the range is shown.
  • Access date for all sources: 2026-11-05. Source URLs are in calc-notes.md in this folder.
