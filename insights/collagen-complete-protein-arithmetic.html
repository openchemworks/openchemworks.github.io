At a glance: what the numbers say

  • Collagen contains zero tryptophan, and zero is the whole story for its quality score. USDA FoodData Central lists tryptophan at 0.0 g per 100 g for gelatin, dry powder (the cooked form of collagen, with the same amino acid profile as collagen peptides). Tryptophan is one of the nine essential amino acids. A protein quality score is set by the lowest essential amino acid ratio, and 0 / 6.6 mg = 0.00. Collagen's DIAAS-style score and its PDCAAS are both zero, not low. Zero.
  • Per 20 g of protein, collagen delivers 3.19 g of essential amino acids. Whey delivers 8.57 g. Same "20 g protein" on two labels, a 2.7-fold difference in the amino acids the buyer cannot make for themselves (3.19 vs 8.57 g, computed from FoodData Central profiles).
  • Leucine: 0.57 g per 20 g of collagen protein, against 1.83 g for whey. Collagen's leucine content is 28.7 mg per gram of protein, 47% of the FAO adult reference pattern of 61 mg/g, before tryptophan is even considered.
  • Even if tryptophan were forgiven, collagen would still fail. Its next-lowest ratio is sulfur amino acids at 0.31 (7.1 mg/g against a 23 mg/g pattern), then isoleucine at 0.45 and leucine at 0.47. Collagen is not one amino acid short of complete. It is short across the board, and rich in exactly the amino acids (glycine 223 mg/g of protein, proline 144 mg/g) that are not essential.
  • Fixing collagen takes a majority of real protein. To lift a collagen blend's chemical score to 1.00 against the FAO adult pattern, the blend needs at least 58% of its protein from egg white, or 78% from whey. Soy isolate, itself sulfur-limited, needs 91%, which is not a collagen product with support. It is a soy product with collagen in it. Any blend where collagen is the majority of the protein cannot reach a complete chemical score with these partners. That is a theorem of these profiles, not an opinion.
  • As collagen's share of a "20 g protein" blend rises from 0% to 50%, the blend's chemical score falls from 1.15 to 0.81 and its essential amino acids per serving fall from 8.57 g to 5.88 g, while the nitrogen-based label can keep printing "20 g protein" the whole way down (Topic 2 explains the nitrogen side).
  • So what: Sell collagen for what it measurably is, the best dietary source of glycine in this dataset (4.45 g per 20 g of protein, 11 times whey's 0.43 g), or blend it as the minority partner and publish the blend math. Do not let it borrow a complete-protein claim its own profile refutes in one line: tryptophan, zero.
  • Why it matters: Protein %DV on a US label is quality-corrected (21 CFR 101.9(c)(7), PDCAAS basis). A PDCAAS of zero means collagen contributes zero corrected grams toward protein %DV when it stands alone. Brands that understand this arithmetic formulate and label accordingly. Brands that do not are one retail audit away from learning it. A total amino acid analysis of your actual product ($230/sample) replaces generic database profiles with your numbers.

Introduction

Collagen is sold by the gram of protein, and on a nitrogen test it earns that gram. This article asks a different question: what does a gram of collagen protein actually deliver in the amino acids a buyer cannot make for themselves, and can any blend in which collagen stays the majority ingredient honestly be called a complete protein? Both questions are answered with arithmetic, not opinion, and the arithmetic is short enough to re-perform from the tables.

The distinction matters commercially, not just scientifically. Protein on a US label is quality-corrected, so a protein with a quality score of zero contributes no corrected grams when it stands alone, whatever the nitrogen count says. A brand that understands this formulates and labels accordingly, selling collagen for the glycine and proline it genuinely delivers in unusual density. A brand that does not is exposed to the first retailer audit that runs the same sums.

The profiles are computed from USDA FoodData Central, with gelatin as the published proxy for collagen peptides, and scored against the FAO adult reference pattern. Read the per-serving table first: it shows what 20 g of each protein delivers. Then read collagen against the pattern, amino acid by amino acid, and finally the blend tables, which solve for how much complete partner protein a collagen blend needs before its score reaches the pattern at all.

Worked example. Gelatin leucine per 20 g protein: FoodData Central lists leucine 2.454 g and protein 85.6 g per 100 g gelatin. (2.454 / 85.6) × 20 = 0.573 g, shown as 0.57 g. Gelatin glycine: (19.049 / 85.6) × 20 = 4.45 g. Essential total: sum the nine essential amino acids per 100 g (0.662 + 1.158 + 2.454 + 3.460 + 0.606 + 1.737 + 1.475 + 0.000 + 2.081 = 13.633 g), then (13.633 / 85.6) × 20 = 3.19 g. Independent cross-check: a published secondary computation from the same FoodData Central entries reports collagen at 0.6 g leucine, 0 g tryptophan, 3.2 g essential amino acids, and 4.4 g glycine per 20 g protein, matching this table to rounding.

Worked example. Sulfur amino acids: methionine 0.606 g + cystine 0.0 g per 100 g gelatin; (0.606 / 85.6) × 1000 = 7.1 mg/g protein; ratio = 7.1 / 23 = 0.31. Eight of nine groups fall below 1.00. Collagen would need every one of them lifted, which no single-ingredient addition of collagen to a diet of itself can do. Blending works only because the partner supplies all eight at once (Table 3).

Table 3: Complementarity math — how much complete protein a collagen blend actually needs (computed)

Blend contents are protein-weighted means of the FoodData Central profiles (Topic 3, Table 1). Score = chemical score (undigested) against the FAO adult pattern. "Minimum partner share" solves for the share at which the last failing group reaches its pattern value.

Partner protein Partner share 50% → blend score (limiting) Minimum partner share for score 1.00 Last group fixed Collagen's maximum share in a "complete" blend
Egg white, dried 0.93 (leucine) 58% Leucine: 28.7 + x(84.3 − 28.7) = 61 → x = 0.581 42%
Whey (sweet, dried profile) 0.81 (histidine) 78% Histidine: 7.7 + x(18.3 − 7.7) = 16 → x = 0.783 22%
Soy protein isolate 0.69 (sulfur AA) 91% Sulfur AA: 7.1 + x(24.6 − 7.1) = 23 → x = 0.909 9%

Worked example. Collagen + egg white at 50:50 protein: tryptophan = (0.0 + 12.3)/2 = 6.15 mg/g, already close to the 6.6 pattern, but leucine = (28.7 + 84.3)/2 = 56.5 mg/g, ratio 56.5/61 = 0.93, so leucine limits and the blend still fails at 50:50. Solving leucine: x = (61 − 28.7)/(84.3 − 28.7) = 32.3/55.6 = 0.581. At 58.1% egg white protein, tryptophan is 7.15 mg/g (ratio 1.08) and every other group exceeds its pattern (full ratios in data.csv), so 58% is the true minimum, set by leucine, not by the famous tryptophan zero. With whey, the binding constraint is different again (histidine), which is why this arithmetic has to be done per partner, not quoted as a slogan.

Digestibility note: these are chemical scores. Applying any digestibility factor below 1.00 to the limiting group (Topic 3, Table 3 method) pushes every minimum share in Table 3 higher. The shares shown are therefore floors, not targets.

Perspectives

Brand: when a collagen claim is honest, and when it borrows protein

"20 g collagen peptides" is an honest claim about an ingredient. "20 g complete protein" for the same scoop is not, and Table 2 is the one-page refutation anyone can re-perform. Collagen's honest strengths are in the same table that convicts its protein claim: glycine density nothing else in this dataset approaches, plus proline at 2.87 g per 20 g protein (12.295/85.6 × 20). Structure, skin, and joint positioning built on glycine and proline does not need a borrowed protein claim.

Formulator: the cheapest complement is the one that fixes the binding constraint

"Cheapest" here is computed as smallest required share, because we did not verify a citable price series for these ingredient grades on the access date, and this program does not rank on unverified prices (gap recorded, as in Topic 3). By share, egg white fixes collagen at 58% partner protein, whey at 78%, soy at 91%. Note the trap: the intuitive fix, "add a plant protein," performs worst, because soy's own limiting group (sulfur amino acids) is collagen's second-worst deficit. Fix the binding constraint (Table 3, last-fixed column), not the famous one.

Consumer-facing science: grams of protein vs usable essential amino acids

A gram of protein is a nitrogen accounting unit. Essential amino acids are the part of that gram the body cannot synthesize. Table 4 shows the two measures separating as collagen share rises: at 100% collagen the accounting unit is intact (20 g) and the usable fraction is 37% of whey's (3.19/8.57). Neither number is a lie. They answer different questions, and only one of them is the question a protein buyer is asking.

Regulatory: collagen in the protein line

US protein %DV is corrected for protein quality (PDCAAS) under 21 CFR 101.9(c)(7). Collagen's PDCAAS is zero because tryptophan is absent, so collagen alone contributes zero quality-corrected grams. In blends, the corrected value follows the blend's limiting amino acid, which Table 3 computes on the chemical side. Supplement Facts protein declarations that count collagen nitrogen at face value sit in the same exposed position Topic 2 maps for spiking, even when the intent is innocent: the nitrogen method cannot distinguish intent either.

  • FAO (2013), Dietary protein quality evaluation in human nutrition, FAO Food and Nutrition Paper 92: adult scoring pattern (His 16, Ile 30, Leu 61, Lys 48, SAA 23, AAA 41, Thr 25, Trp 6.6, Val 40 mg/g protein) and score definition (lowest ratio; DIAAS not truncated). Accessed November 5, 2026.
  • 21 CFR 101.9(c)(7): protein %DV corrected by PDCAAS. Accessed November 5, 2026.
  • Cross-check: an independent published computation from the same FoodData Central SR Legacy entries (gelatin 169599; whey 171283; egg white 172204; soy isolate 174276) reporting, per 20 g protein, collagen 0.6 g leucine / 0 g tryptophan / 3.2 g essential amino acids / 4.4 g glycine and whey 1.8 g leucine / 8.6 g essential amino acids. Agreement with Table 1 is within rounding on every cross-checked figure. Accessed November 5, 2026.
  • Formulas: per-20 g values = (g per 100 g / protein g per 100 g) × 20; content (mg/g) = (g per 100 g / protein g per 100 g) × 1000; blend content = protein-weighted mean; minimum partner share solves content equation for the last failing group, then verifies all groups (data.csv). Worksheet: data.csv. Notes: calc-notes.md.
  • Limitations: gelatin is the FoodData Central proxy for collagen peptides; commercial hydrolyzed collagen profiles are expected to be close (same source protein) but a specific product's profile is a measurement, not this table. FoodData Central's gelatin entry publishes cystine as 0.0 and no hydroxyproline value (see Topic 2); hydroxyproline is non-essential and does not affect any score here, but total amino acid sums for gelatin are understated for that reason. Chemical scores ignore digestibility; see Topic 3 for what that omission costs.
