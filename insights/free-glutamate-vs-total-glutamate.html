Free vs total glutamate: which test answers your question

Published October 16, 2026Updated November 19, 20268 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Order free glutamate (AA-1, $150) if your question is about the food as eaten: savoury taste, seasoning strength, an "no added MSG" claim. Order total glutamate (AA-T, $230) if your question is about the protein itself. Free glutamate is measured directly. Total glutamate is measured after acid hydrolysis, so it is larger and includes glutamine converted to glutamate. Ordering the wrong one is the most common way people waste money on glutamate testing.

The distinction, in plain terms

Glutamate is the form in food of the amino acid glutamic acid. Some of it is free: dissolved, unattached, available to taste buds and to a measuring instrument without further chemistry. The rest is bound, built into protein chains the way beads are built into a necklace. Bound glutamate contributes to nutrition as protein, but it does not taste of glutamate until the protein is digested or chemically broken down.

A free amino acid test measures only the free fraction. A total amino acid test first hydrolyses the sample in 6 N hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 hours, which releases the amino acids from the protein, then measures everything. Total therefore includes free. Hydrolysis also converts glutamine to glutamate and asparagine to aspartate, so the total result is reported as Glx and Asx and is never a photograph of the original food.

Which question do you actually have?

If you are adjusting a seasoning blend, comparing two sauce recipes as eaten, or asking how much MSG equivalent a serving delivers, you have a free question. If you are characterising a protein ingredient or supplying a total profile someone has asked for in writing, you have a total question. If you are unsure, send this sentence to a lab: "I want to know X, as eaten or as protein. Which test is that?" A lab that asks what decision the number feeds is advising you.

How each is measured here

Free glutamate sits inside the AA-1 free amino acid panel: 19 amino acids plus ammonia, by AQC (AccQ-Tag) derivatization with HPLC and dual UV and fluorescence detection, in µM and mg/L. The sample is prepared without hydrolysis. Seeing the whole free profile alongside glutamate shows whether glutamate dominates the free fraction. Total glutamate sits inside AA-T, 16 amino acids after hydrolysis, $230 per sample with hydrolysis included. Powders that need extraction add PREP-1 at +$30 per sample. For a salty, dark or oily blend, a spike-recovery check (PREP-2, $200 per assay, once per formulation, waived at 20 or more samples) tells you whether the matrix distorts the reading. Free and bound glutamate in eight foods

The ledger below keeps the two numbers apart, food by food. Total glutamate is glutamic acid from USDA FoodData Central (SR Legacy), which comes from acid hydrolysis and so includes glutamine converted to glutamate. Free glutamate is from published free amino acid food composition measurements. Bound is total minus free, and free % is free divided by total. Free and total come from different samples and producers, so each row is an estimate for a food class, not a measurement of one specimen. Report them as ranges when you cite them.

Milk is the control case: its free glutamate is published as about 2 mg per 100 g, a one-digit value, so its free share is under 1% and the exact ratio is not meaningful. Soy sauce is the opposite case, but its free value (1,246 mg) comes from a Korean soy sauce table while the total (1,579 mg) is for US shoyu in FoodData Central, so that 78.9% pairs two different products and is shown only as an indication that fermentation has already done much of the hydrolysis.

Worked example, Parmesan. Total: FDC 170848, glutamic acid 8.209 g/100 g = 8,209 mg/100 g. Free: 1,200 mg/100 g (a published compilation; values across compilations run from 1,200 to 1,680). Bound = 8,209 minus 1,200 = 7,009 mg/100 g. Free % = 1,200 / 8,209 = 14.6%. Parmesan holds 6.6 times the free glutamate of cheddar (1,200 / 182) on 1.45 times the total (8,209 / 5,675). Parmesan's savoury reputation is a free glutamate story, not a total glutamate story.

The reporting basis changes the headline too. The same free glutamate reads as glutamic acid (x 1.000), anhydrous MSG (x 1.149) or MSG monohydrate (x 1.272), a spread of 27%.

Limits that apply to both numbers

Neither number tells you how much MSG powder was added to a recipe, because glutamate arrives from many ingredients and the tests measure the combined result. A "no added MSG" claim is about addition, and the ledger does not contradict it: a product with Parmesan can honestly say no MSG was added while carrying natural free glutamate. Neither number assesses safety. A single sample describes that sample, and a reformulation deserves a retest if the number still carries a claim.

Frequently asked questions

Is total glutamate the "real" amount and free just a portion?
Both are real; they measure different fractions. Total includes free plus protein-bound, and the total from acid hydrolysis also includes glutamine converted to glutamate. Calling total "real" assumes the protein question is your question.

Will total always be much higher?
It will be higher where the food contains substantial protein. In the ledger below, the free share runs from under 1% of total glutamate in milk to about 41% in dried shiitake, so measure the sample you care about.

Can I convert free glutamate to MSG?
Yes, with a stated form, anhydrous or monohydrate, using molar-mass ratios. The reporting-bases article works the conversion.

Do I ever need both?
Occasionally, when you need to know both the as-eaten free level and the protein composition. Free plus total on the same powder is AA-1 $150 plus AA-T $230, plus $30 if the powder needs extraction.

What about glutamine on my report?
Free glutamine is reported separately in AA-1. After acid hydrolysis, glutamine is converted to glutamate and reported with it as Glx, so a total report cannot give native glutamine or native glutamate separately.
