ESTIMATES, NOT MEASUREMENTS — read this first. Every score on this page labeled "DIAAS-style estimate" or "chemical score" is an estimate assembled from published components: amino acid profiles from USDA FoodData Central, reference patterns from FAO (2013), and digestibility assumptions stated in the tables. True DIAAS is a measured value from ileal digestibility studies in humans or pigs. Nobody at OpenChemWorks ran those studies. Where a published measured DIAAS exists (Mathai, Liu & Stein, 2017), we print it separately and label it "measured (published)" so the two kinds of number can never be confused.

At a glance: what the numbers say

  • Gelatin (collagen) scores 0.00, at every age pattern and every digestibility assumption. Its tryptophan content in FoodData Central is 0 mg per gram of protein, and a score built on the lowest ratio is zero when any indispensable amino acid is zero. Arithmetic in Topic 4 develops this in full.
  • Among the eight ingredients we computed, only wheat flour (apart from gelatin at 0.00, above) falls below the FAO "good quality" line on chemistry alone. Wheat's chemical score (older child/adult pattern) is 0.46, limited by lysine at 22.1 mg/g protein against a 48 mg/g pattern. Split peas score 0.88 (limited by sulfur amino acids). Everything dairy, egg, and soy scores above 1.00 before any digestibility correction.
  • Digestibility is where plant scores are decided. Soy isolate's chemical score is 1.07 (sulfur amino acids limiting). Apply a 0.85 digestibility factor to the limiting amino acids and the DIAAS-style estimate is 0.91. Apply 0.75 and it is 0.80. Same chemistry, three different claims, decided entirely by an assumption, which is why this page prints the assumption next to every estimate.
  • A 50:50 wheat + pea protein blend (by protein) scores 1.03, higher than either ingredient alone (wheat 0.46, pea 0.88). Wheat brings the sulfur amino acids peas lack (38.9 vs 20.2 mg/g); peas bring the lysine wheat lacks (76.6 vs 22.1 mg/g). The minimum pea share that lifts the blend's lysine to the adult pattern is 47.5% of protein. Blend math, not marketing, is how plant proteins become complete.
  • Published measured DIAAS and our computed chemical scores rank ingredients the same way. Mathai et al. (2017), measuring ileal digestibility in pigs, found dairy proteins at or above 100 on the young-child pattern (milk protein concentrate 120, whey concentrate 107, whey isolate 100), soy isolate at 84, and pea concentrate at 62. Our undigested chemical scores are higher, as they must be, because digestion only removes amino acids. The gap between the two columns is the digestibility cost, made visible.
  • So what: If you are formulating or substantiating a protein-quality claim, the claim rests on three separable numbers: the amino acid profile (measurable today, on your actual ingredient, for $230), the reference pattern (published, free), and digestibility (borrowed from literature, and the weakest link unless your ingredient is the one that was measured). Know which of the three your claim is actually standing on.
  • Why it matters: A total amino acid analysis (AA-T) is the input measurement every score needs, and it is the only one of the three inputs you can own outright for your specific lot. Literature profiles describe a generic ingredient. Your claim describes your product.

Introduction

Protein quality claims are usually quoted as a single score, as if the score were a property of the ingredient. This article takes the score apart. A quality score stands on three separable inputs: an amino acid profile, a reference pattern that says how much of each indispensable amino acid a gram of protein should carry, and a digestibility figure that says how much of what is present is actually absorbed. The question the article answers is which of those three inputs each published score is really standing on, and how far the score moves when an assumption changes.

That matters to anyone formulating a product or substantiating a claim, because only one of the three inputs can be measured on your own ingredient: its amino acid profile. The reference pattern is published and free. Digestibility is almost always borrowed from literature on a related material, and for mid-scoring plant proteins that borrowed assumption, not the chemistry, decides whether the score clears 1.00.

The profiles are computed from USDA FoodData Central entries, the patterns are those published by FAO, and the measured comparison values are the published DIAAS results of Mathai and colleagues, kept in a separate column throughout so that estimates and measurements are never confused. Read the chemical scores first: they are the ceiling every estimate starts from. Then read the scenario estimates beside the measured values, with the digestibility assumption printed next to each one, and finish with the worked blend, which shows how two incomplete proteins can complete each other.

Worked example. Soy isolate estimate at SID 0.85: 1.07 × 0.85 = 0.91. At SID 0.75: 1.07 × 0.75 = 0.80. Sensitivity: a 0.10 change in the digestibility assumption moves the estimate by 0.11 (1.07 × 0.10), which is larger than the entire gap between "good" (75–99) band boundaries for several ingredients in this table. That is the finding: for mid-scoring plant proteins, the digestibility assumption is the score.

Caveats printed with the table, not under it: (1) our ingredient proxies differ from Mathai's test materials (whole split peas are not pea protein concentrate; dried sweet whey is not whey isolate), so the estimate and measured columns are expected to differ in both directions; (2) Mathai's values use the young-child pattern, ours use the >3 y pattern, which is less demanding; (3) FAO bands: DIAAS ≥ 100 "excellent/high," 75–99 "good," below 75 no protein-quality claim. PDCAAS, the score FDA uses for %DV, truncates at 1.00 and uses fecal rather than ileal digestibility and the older preschool pattern, so a PDCAAS of 1.00 and a DIAAS-style estimate of 0.91 for the same ingredient are not a contradiction. They are different instruments.

Worked blend: wheat + pea (computed)

Blend contents are protein-weighted means of Table 1. At 50% wheat protein + 50% pea protein: lysine = (22.1 + 76.6)/2 = 49.4 mg/g (score 49.4/48 = 1.03); SAA = (38.9 + 20.2)/2 = 29.6 mg/g (score 1.29); valine = (40.2 + 44.8)/2 = 42.5 (score 1.06); all other groups score higher. Blend chemical score = 1.03, lysine-limited, against 0.46 and 0.88 for the components. Minimum pea share to reach lysine 48 mg/g: solve 22.1 + x(76.6 − 22.1) = 48, so x = 25.9/54.5 = 0.475. At 47.5% pea protein the blend's next-lowest group is valine at 1.06, so lysine remains limiting exactly at the pattern. One blend per cycle will be added to this section.

Perspectives

Formulator: pair by limiting amino acid, not by marketing category

Table 1 reads as a pairing chart. Legumes are lysine-rich and SAA-poor; cereals are the reverse. The wheat + pea computation above is the template: identify each ingredient's limiting group, check the partner's surplus in exactly that group, solve for the share. Soy isolate needs no partner on chemistry alone at the adult pattern (1.07); its problem is digestibility evidence, not a partner.

Label: PDCAAS vs DIAAS-style, where they disagree

FDA protein %DV still runs on PDCAAS, truncated at 1.00. Truncation hides real differences at the top (milk at 1.49 chemical score and egg white at 1.38 both print as 1.00) and the fecal digestibility basis flatters some plant proteins relative to ileal scoring. If your label claim and your marketing claim use different scores, this table is where the discrepancy will be found, by you or by someone else.

Ingredient buyer: score per dollar — data gap, reported honestly

A score-per-dollar ranking requires reliable published prices for each ingredient grade. We verified public price reporting for dairy ingredients (USDA Dairy Market News covers whey grades) but did not verify equivalent public reporting for pea and soy ingredient grades on the access date. The per-dollar table is therefore not published in this version rather than built on spot marketplace listings. Gap recorded; it closes when a citable price series for plant ingredients is verified.

Skeptic: how much of this ranking survives a different assumption?

Change the pattern (adult to child) and whey drops from 1.15 to 0.92, soy from 1.07 to 0.91 (Table 2). Change the digestibility assumption by 0.10 and mid-table estimates move by roughly 0.10 (Table 3). Change nothing and gelatin is still 0.00 and wheat is still last. The top and bottom of the ranking are robust; the middle is assumption-driven, and this page's estimates should be cited with their assumptions attached or not cited at all.

Patterns cross-verified against the IDF factsheet summary of FAO 2013 and the Stein laboratory's published pattern footnotes. Accessed November 5, 2026.
  • USDA FoodData Central, SR Legacy, via the FDC API: FDC 172195, 172204, 172188, 171283, 174276, 172428, 169761, 169599 (protein and amino acids per 100 g). https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/ — accessed November 5, 2026.
  • Mathai, J.K., Liu, Y. & Stein, H.H. (2017), British Journal of Nutrition: measured DIAAS in growing pigs for dairy and plant proteins (young-child pattern: milk protein concentrate 120, whey protein concentrate 107, whey protein isolate 100, soy flour 89, soy protein isolate 84, pea protein concentrate 62; dairy proteins first-limited by histidine or sulfur amino acids, soy and pea by sulfur amino acids), via the University of Illinois Stein laboratory publications and published summaries of the paper. Accessed November 5, 2026.
  • Formulas: content (mg/g protein) = (g per 100 g food / protein g per 100 g food) × 1000; ratio = content / pattern; chemical score = minimum ratio; DIAAS-style estimate = chemical score × assumed SID of limiting group; blend content = protein-weighted mean. Worksheet: data.csv. Notes: calc-notes.md.
  • Limitations: chemical scores ignore digestibility entirely and are ceilings, not quality claims. Scenario estimates apply one uniform SID to the limiting group only; true DIAAS applies a measured SID per amino acid. Ingredient proxies (whole foods for commercial isolates/concentrates) are named in every table. FoodData Central entries are SR Legacy values for generic foods, not specifications for any commercial ingredient.
