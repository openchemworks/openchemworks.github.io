Label claims for small food brands: what testing backs them up

Published October 20, 20266 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Testing backs up a label claim only when the test measures exactly what the claim says, in the product as sold, on the basis the claim uses. For amino acid claims, that means matching three things before you print: the fraction (free or total), the panel (which amino acids were measured), and the reporting basis (per gram, per 100 grams, or per serving, and in which form). This page sorts small-brand claims into those an amino acid report can support, those it can support only with careful wording, and those it cannot support — so you can fix the wording before labels are printed, not after.

Claims a free amino acid report can back up

A statement of measured content is the strongest claim a report supports: that a named amino acid was measured at a stated amount in the tested product, expressed on the same basis as the report. The panel here, AA-1, measures free amino acids — those already available in the food as eaten — covering 19 amino acids plus ammonia by AQC (AccQ-Tag) derivatization with HPLC-UV. A claim inside that sentence is defensible: name the amino acid, use the free fraction word, copy the basis exactly, and keep the batch identity with the report.

Free glutamate content is the example most small makers need. If your seasoning's free glutamate was measured, you can state that result, and express it as an MSG equivalent if — and only if — the report states the form used, anhydrous or monohydrate, because the two convert differently. The reporting-bases companion article shows the conversion with PubChem-cited molar masses. What you cannot do is convert a free glutamate result into a claim about how much MSG was added in production: the test measures glutamate from all ingredient sources combined; only your recipe record knows the added portion.

Consistency language is also supportable in modest form. If you test successive batches the same way, you can truthfully describe your checking practice. That is a claim about what you do, backed by reports you hold — keep it to what the reports show, for the batches tested.

Claims that need the total test — or different wording

Any claim about the protein's amino acid makeup belongs to total amino acid analysis, AA-T, not the free panel. AA-T hydrolyses the sample in 6 N hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 hours, releasing amino acids bound in protein before measurement, and costs $230 per sample against $150 per sample for liquids and $180 for powders ($150 plus $30 powder preparation) for AA-1, with first-time customers at $150 flat and $125 per sample at 25 or more samples per month. If your draft label talks about the amino acids in the protein, either commission AA-T or rewrite the claim to talk about the free fraction you measured. The free-versus-total companion article will keep you honest about which is which.

Completeness language needs the same discipline. Because cysteine is excluded from the AA-1 free panel — it requires a separate AA-3 workup — and because the extended AA-2 compounds (GABA, ornithine, citrulline, taurine, hydroxyproline) are not routinely included, no report from this panel supports a claim of a complete amino acid profile, and any claim naming cysteine or taurine needs its own evidence from an appropriate workup. Shorten the claim to the panel you bought. "Measured for 19 free amino acids" is smaller than "complete profile," and it has the virtue of being true.

Basis-shifting is the third wording trap. A result reported per 100 grams does not become more impressive re-expressed per serving unless the serving really is 100 grams. Convert carefully, show your working, and never let a designer round a measured value into a rounder claim. Matrix caveat: for a complex blend, a result without matrix-effect qualification — the spike-recovery check on your actual sample, priced per sample and matrix on inquiry, with a recent single-sample seasoning project at $350 — is a weaker foundation for a printed claim than a qualified result. If the number will be printed where customers can rely on it, print from the qualified version.

Claims this testing cannot back up

Amino acid analysis does not support safety claims, health or function claims, or quality superlatives. It does not show a product is safe, supports sleep, recovery, or any other outcome, is superior to a competitor, or complies with a regulation. Those claims need different evidence, different experts, and in some cases are not yours to make at all on a food label. This page makes none of them, and an OpenChemWorks report will not make them for you.

Status matters as much as scope. OpenChemWorks, in Davis, California, is not GLP, GMP, or ISO/IEC 17025 accredited; it is validating now and reserving pilot slots; and its results are for the maker's own decisions, not regulatory submission. A claim implying accredited certification behind the number misdescribes the evidence even if the number is accurate. If a retailer or platform requires accredited testing, that requirement governs — obtain it in writing and commission accordingly.

A pre-print checklist

For each draft claim, ask: Does my report name this amino acid? Does it use the same fraction — free or total? Does it use the same basis and, for MSG equivalents, the same named form? Was the tested sample the product as sold, from a batch I can identify? Was the matrix checked for a complex blend? And does the claim stay silent about safety, health outcomes, and accreditation? Six yeses, and the claim matches its evidence. Any no is a rewrite, a retest, or a deletion — all cheaper before printing than after.

Your next step

Send your draft claim sentences, before you print, to info@openchemworks.com with a description of the product and its form. Ask which test, if any, backs each sentence, and the all-in price. Deleting one unsupported sentence now is the cheapest label revision you will ever make.

Frequently asked questions

Can I say "contains glutamate" without a number?
A content statement still needs evidence glutamate was measured in the product as sold. If you have not measured it, you are relying on ingredient generalities, not your product. Measure first, then word it.

Can I use a supplier's amino acid data for my label?
Supplier data describes the supplier's ingredient, not your finished, mixed, possibly cooked product. It is background, not a measurement.

What if my claim was true for one batch?
It describes that batch. Keep the claim tied to a recipe and checking practice you maintain, and retest when the recipe or a key supplier changes.

Does "natural" or "no added MSG" follow from a glutamate result?
No. A free glutamate measurement cannot separate added MSG from glutamate from other ingredients, so it neither proves nor disproves an "added" claim. Your recipe and sourcing records carry that claim, not the chromatogram.
