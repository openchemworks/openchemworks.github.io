At a glance: what the numbers say

  • Two major US reference laboratories disagree enough to flip interpretations. Across 24 amino acids, the adult intervals published by Mayo Clinic Laboratories and ARUP Laboratories overlap by a median of only 73.6% of their combined span (our computation; mean 69.4%). For 3 of 24 analytes the overlap is under half the combined span.
  • Cystine is the extreme case. Mayo's adult interval is 8 to 310 µmol/L (width 302); ARUP's is 10 to 65 µmol/L (width 55). The intervals overlap by only 18.2% of their union, and Mayo's is 5.49 times wider. A plasma cystine of 150 µmol/L is inside Mayo's interval and more than double ARUP's upper limit.
  • Threonine and phenylalanine also split. Threonine: Mayo 73 to 325 vs ARUP 60 to 190 µmol/L (overlap 44.2% of union). Phenylalanine: Mayo 45 to 106 vs ARUP 30 to 82 µmol/L (overlap 48.7%).
  • Mayo's intervals are wider as a rule, not by exception. Median adult interval width: Mayo 108.5 µmol/L vs ARUP 90.0 µmol/L. Mayo is the wider interval for 21 of the 24 analytes we could compare.
  • The age bands do not line up either. Mayo's adult band is ≥18 years; ARUP's broad band is ≥1 year. Part of the "disagreement" is two different populations wearing the same label, "adult," in casual citation. Glutamine shows it: Mayo ≥18 years 447 to 774 vs ARUP ≥1 year 380 to 680 µmol/L; midpoints 610.5 vs 530.0, a 15.2% gap.
  • Some analytes are genuinely stable across labs. Tyrosine overlaps 94.9% of union (Mayo 36 to 113, ARUP 35 to 110) and methionine 92.6% (Mayo 13 to 40, ARUP 15 to 40). Agreement exists; it is analyte-specific, which is the point of a harmonized table.
  • So what: Before you call a plasma result high or low, name the interval you are judging it against. For cystine, threonine, phenylalanine, lysine, and ornithine, the laboratory whose interval you borrow can change the conclusion more than the biology did.
  • Why it matters: Research groups routinely compare plasma values generated in one lab against reference intervals published by another, or pool cohorts whose "normal" flags came from different intervals. This table shows, analyte by analyte, where that is defensible and where it manufactures abnormality.

Introduction

When is a plasma amino acid result normal? The honest answer depends on the reference interval it is judged against, and that interval is part of the method, not a universal constant. This article puts the adult intervals published by Mayo Clinic Laboratories and ARUP Laboratories side by side for 24 amino acids, harmonized to the same units, and computes where they agree and where the same result would be flagged differently.

That matters to anyone buying or using plasma results. Research groups routinely compare values generated in one laboratory against intervals published by another, or pool cohorts whose normal flags came from different intervals. Where the intervals diverge, that practice can manufacture abnormality, or hide it, without any change in the underlying biology.

Read the harmonized table as an agreement map. For each amino acid it lists both intervals, their widths, and the overlap as a share of their combined span, so the discordant zones are visible analyte by analyte. The perspectives that follow then take up what the disagreement means for researchers, for study design, and for a buyer deciding what to ask before sending plasma.

Worked example (Table 1, cystine). Mayo width = 310 − 8 = 302. ARUP width = 65 − 10 = 55. Overlap = min(310, 65) − max(8, 10) = 65 − 10 = 55. Union = max(310, 65) − min(8, 10) = 310 − 8 = 302. Overlap % = 55 ÷ 302 × 100 = 18.2%. Width ratio = 302 ÷ 55 = 5.49. Median width: the 24 Mayo widths sort to 12th and 13th values of 102 and 115, median (102 + 115)/2 = 108.5 µmol/L; the ARUP widths give a median of 90.0 µmol/L (both re-performable from data.csv).

Worked example (a flipped call). Plasma threonine 250 µmol/L: inside Mayo's 73 to 325 (normal), above ARUP's upper limit of 190 by 60 µmol/L, i.e., 31.6% over (250 ÷ 190 = 1.316). Same specimen, same number, opposite flag. No new biology is needed to produce this discrepancy; only a change of reference interval.

Perspectives

Researcher: what "abnormal" means when intervals disagree. Treat the interval as part of the method, cited with the same care as the instrument. In a manuscript, "elevated (Mayo ≥18 y interval)" and "elevated (ARUP ≥1 y interval)" are different claims for at least 5 of these 24 analytes at plausible values. Our table gives you the discordant zones (the non-overlapping parts) analyte by analyte in data.csv.

Analytical: method as a cause of interval shift. Both laboratories use LC-MS/MS for these panels (Mayo: HILIC LC-MS/MS on underivatized amino acids per its method description; ARUP: quantitative LC-MS/MS). Same method family, materially different intervals, which points the explanation at reference populations, age banding, fasting requirements, and sample handling rather than detection chemistry. Mayo requires a 4-hour fast; ARUP states a fasting specimen is preferred for adults. Non-fasting and delayed separation move glutamine, glutamate, and the branched-chain amino acids first.

Study design: fasting and handling, with numbers. Compare like with like inside each source before comparing across sources: Mayo's own glutamine interval moves from 356 to 857 (<24 months) to 447 to 774 (≥18 years), a lower limit 25.5% higher in adults (447 ÷ 356 = 1.255). Age alone, inside one laboratory's system, shifts limits by more than many interventions researchers test. Pooling pediatric-flagged and adult-flagged results without re-flagging against one interval is a design error this table makes visible.

Lab buyer: what to ask before sending plasma. Ask four things: which interval (whose, which age band) will my results be flagged against; fasting requirement; separation and freezing window (Mayo: centrifuge within 4 hours refrigerated, freeze; ARUP: separate ASAP or within 2 hours, freeze immediately); and whether the report carries the interval on the same page as the result. If you are buying research-use plasma analysis rather than a clinical panel, ask whether you get the raw concentrations so you can apply your own harmonized interval. With us, you do.

Methods & Sources

  • Mayo Clinic Laboratories (accessed November 5, 2026): Amino Acids, Quantitative, Plasma (test AAQP), LC-MS/MS; reference values in nmol/mL for <24 months, 2 to 17 years, ≥18 years; fasting 4 hours required. Values transcribed from the Mayo test catalog as republished in full by MU Health Care Laboratory Test Catalog (performing laboratory: Mayo Clinic Laboratories, Rochester).
  • ARUP Laboratories (accessed November 5, 2026): Amino Acids Quantitative by LC-MS/MS, Plasma (test 2009389); reference intervals in µmol/L by age band; adults fasting preferred. The ≥1 year band is used as ARUP's adult-comparable band and is labeled as such throughout; ARUP does not publish a separate ≥18 year band for this test, which is itself a harmonization finding.
  • Unit identity: 1 nmol/mL = (10⁻⁹ mol)/(10⁻³ L) = 10⁻⁶ mol/L = 1 µmol/L. Mayo values are therefore numerically unchanged in µmol/L.
  • Formulas: width = high − low; midpoint = (low + high)/2; overlap = max(0, min(highs) − max(lows)); union = max(highs) − min(lows); overlap % = 100 × overlap/union. One-sided intervals (<13, ≤15) were entered with low = 0 and are flagged in data.csv by construction; their widths are upper-bound spans, not central intervals.
  • Limitations: two sources only in this first release; intervals are as published on the access date and both laboratories revise them; ARUP's asparagine (20 to 80) and histidine (50 to 130) are published as single all-age intervals. Cystine naming is not harmonized in the sources themselves (Mayo lists "Cystine (Cys)" with an unusually wide ≥18 year span of 8 to 310); we report it as published and flag it rather than correcting another laboratory's table.
