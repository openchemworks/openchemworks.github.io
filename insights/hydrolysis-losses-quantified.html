Hydrolysis Losses, Quantified: What the Method Does to the Numbers

Summary: key findings

  • Tryptophan reads zero, and that is the method, not your product. In standard 6 N HCl hydrolysis at 110 °C, tryptophan is destroyed. A total-amino-acid report with no tryptophan line, or a zero, tells you the lab ran acid hydrolysis without a separate alkaline step. It tells you nothing about your sample's tryptophan.
  • Asparagine and glutamine disappear by design, into other lines. Acid hydrolysis quantitatively deamidates Asn to aspartate and Gln to glutamate. Reports therefore carry Asx and Glx, not the original amides. If a report lists separate asparagine and glutamine values from a hydrolysed run, ask how.
  • Serine and threonine read low by a published, correctable amount. The literature puts their loss at 24 h at 5–10% (Rutherfurd & Gilani 2016) to 5–15% (hydrolysis reviews and method guides). One published correction set (TNO, reported in a 2024 Frontiers in Nutrition review of human-milk amino acid data) uses factors of 1.10 for serine and 1.05 for threonine, which implies the uncorrected report sits about 9% low on serine and 5% low on threonine.
  • Valine and isoleucine read low for the opposite reason: they are still locked up. Hydrophobic bonds release slowly; a 24 h hydrolysis recovers roughly 80–90% of valine and isoleucine from resistant cores, and the same TNO correction set uses 1.07 (valine) and 1.08 (isoleucine). Longer is not simply better: extending hydrolysis to free more Val/Ile destroys more Ser/Thr. That trade-off is built into every total-amino-acid number ever reported.
  • Worked on a real profile, the distortions are larger than most lab-to-lab differences people argue about. Applying the published ranges to the WPC80 profile (true serine 4.08 g/100 g), a 24 h acid run should observe 3.47–3.88 g serine, not 4.08. Two labs, one correcting and one not, can differ by about 10% on serine while both doing competent work.

So what: When you compare total-amino-acid reports between labs, or against a specification, first ask which corrections were applied and how tryptophan, cysteine, and methionine were handled. Without those three answers, a 5–10% discrepancy on several lines is uninterpretable.

Why it matters: Total amino acid analysis is the measurement behind protein content claims, blend calculations, and DIAAS-style scores. Its known, published biases are not a scandal; they are a budget. Buyers who know the budget read reports correctly. Labs that publish their budget, as this article does for the method class, are the ones whose numbers can be trusted to mean something specific.

Introduction

Every total-amino-acid report is produced by hydrolysing the sample first, and the hydrolysis itself changes the numbers. This article quantifies that change: which amino acids read low after a standard acid hydrolysis, which are converted into other lines on the report, and which is destroyed outright, so that a zero on the page says something about the method and nothing about the product.

It matters because total amino acid analysis is the measurement behind protein content claims, blend calculations, and protein-quality scores. Two competent labs can report materially different values for the same sample purely because one applies published corrections and the other does not. Without knowing which corrections were applied, a discrepancy between reports cannot be interpreted at all.

The table that follows is computed, not measured: published loss ranges and a published set of correction factors are applied to a real whey protein concentrate profile from the U.S. Dairy Export Council Reference Manual, to show what a competent run should observe for each amino acid. For the two sulfur amino acids no single defensible published loss figure could be sourced, so none is printed; inventing a tidy percentage for them is exactly the failure this program exists to avoid.

Read each row as a small budget: a true value, a fate under acid hydrolysis, the range a run should observe, and the correction that closes the gap. A closing section then covers the error that happens before any chemistry at all, when the subsample taken from a heterogeneous powder may not represent the batch it came from.

Worked example, serine scenario: true 4.08 g/100 g × (1 − 0.10) = 3.67 g observed. Applying the TNO factor in reverse: 4.08 ÷ 1.10 = 3.71 g, the value a lab that does not correct would report if its loss matched the TNO assumption. The two scenario numbers (3.67, 3.71) differ because they come from different published sources; the article shows both rather than averaging them into a false precision.

Gap note, stated plainly: for cysteine and methionine, the sources fetched for this version (Singh et al. 2014 review; Rutherfurd & Gilani 2016) establish the direction and the remedy (performic oxidation) but do not give a single defensible percentage loss for a standard 24 h run. This table therefore does not print one. Inventing a tidy percentage for the two sulfur amino acids is exactly the failure this program exists to avoid.

Perspectives

Buyer. A report that omits Trp and Cys is not incomplete work; it is acid hydrolysis described honestly. Price the add-ons (alkaline hydrolysis for Trp, performic oxidation for sulfur amino acids) when you need those lines, and treat any lab that reports all 20 from a single acid run with suspicion.

Analytical. The Ser/Thr-versus-Val/Ile trade-off means hydrolysis time is a choice about which error you prefer. Time-course hydrolysis (24/48/72 h, extrapolate Ser/Thr back to zero time and Val/Ile to infinite time) is the published way to have both, at the cost of three runs.

Inter-lab. Correction practice is a hidden variable in lab-to-lab comparisons. Before disputing a result, exchange correction factors. A lab applying 1.10 to serine and a lab applying none will differ by 10% on that line forever, reproducibly.

Honesty. These limits apply to our own total-amino-acid service. Our reports state the hydrolysis conditions and the corrections applied, and Trp/Cys/Met are reported only when the protective steps for them were run. The numbers in this article are the method's published budget, and our reports are written against it.

"Before Hydrolysis: The Subsample Can Be the Biggest Loss of All"

Placement: fold into Topic 15's article, after its expected-observation table, before Perspectives. Status of this section: computed model, honestly labelled. Topic 20's standalone gate report (topic-20/GATE-REPORT.md, November 5, 2026) records why this material is a section and not an article: Gy's sampling constants are not published for amino acid matrices, so what follows uses a transparent particle-count model, not a calibrated Gy prediction. Primary CTA for this section (inherited from Topic 20): sample submission

Summary of this section

  • Hydrolysis losses (Topic 15's subject) are typically single-digit percentages per amino acid. Subsampling error can be larger, and it happens first. In a particle-count model built on published dairy-powder particle sizes, a 0.2 g subsample of a coarse powder (top size 382 µm, a real commercial WPC80 value) whose critical component is 5% of particles carries a sampling RSD of 5.2%, before any chemistry.
  • Particle size, not mass, is the lever. Particle mass scales with diameter cubed: the sample mass needed for a 1% sampling RSD is 0.021 g at 60 µm median size vs 5.40 g at 382 µm, a 258-fold difference (=(382/60)³).
  • Grinding is the cheapest precision in the whole measurement. Milling a coarse blend toward 60 µm cuts the required sample mass for the same sampling error by two orders of magnitude; no instrument upgrade does that.

So what: if two labs report different amino acid numbers for "the same" powder, check the subsample before you blame the hydrolysis. A beautiful chromatogram can describe the wrong 200 mg perfectly.

Why it matters: you control this variable completely. What you ship, how it is blended, and whether the lab grinds and composites before taking its aliquot decide whether the result describes your batch or your scoop.

The model, shown

Sampling theory (Gy, 1979; published simplified form S²_FE = K·d^α / M_S) says sampling variance falls with sample mass and rises steeply with particle top size. The sampling constant K is material-specific and is not published for amino acid matrices, so we do not use Gy's calibrated form. Instead we compute the elementary particle-count limit that sits underneath it:

  • Particle mass m = ρ × (π/6) × d³, with ρ = 0.974 g/cm³ (published apparent density of whey protein isolate particles) and spherical particles.
  • Number of particles in a sample of mass M: N = M / m.
  • If the component you care about is a fraction p of the particles, counting statistics give RSD = √((1−p) / (N·p)). We use p = 0.05 (a minor blend component, e.g., an added amino acid or a second protein in a blend) as the worked case.

Computed table (p = 0.05; full worksheet in topic-20/data.csv):

Particle size d Particle mass RSD at 0.2 g RSD at 1 g RSD at 10 g Mass needed for 1% RSD
60 µm (fine WPI-like; published WPI D50 = 58.5 µm) 0.00011 mg 0.32% 0.14% 0.05% 0.021 g
250 µm (coarse powder) 0.00797 mg 2.75% 1.23% 0.39% 1.51 g
382 µm (coarsest commercial WPC80 in Onwulata et al. 2004) 0.02843 mg 5.20% 2.32% 0.73% 5.40 g

Worked example: d = 382 µm = 0.0382 cm. m = 0.974 × 0.5236 × (0.0382)³ = 2.84 × 10⁻⁵ g. A 0.2 g sample holds N = 0.2 / 2.84×10⁻⁵ ≈ 7,035 particles. RSD = √(0.95 / (7,035 × 0.05)) = √(0.00270) = 5.2%. Compare Topic 15's hydrolysis corrections: for several amino acids the entire method correction is smaller than this subsampling spread. The two error sources also differ in kind: hydrolysis losses are systematic and partly correctable; subsampling error is random per scoop and is not correctable after the fact.

Limits, in the same paragraph: real powders are size distributions, not single diameters (using the coarse end is the conservative choice, and Gy's formalism likewise keys on top size); particles are not spheres of one density; segregation during shipping can make the effective p vary through the container, which makes real error worse than this random-mixing model, never better. Treat the table as a floor, not a prediction.

What to send (the practical table this section exists for)

Your matrix Send Why, in one line
Fine powder (protein powders, peptones) 5–10 g, container mixed before the lab subsamples At ≤60 µm, even 0.2 g counts millions of particles (model: 1.8 million at 0.2 g); the risk is segregation in transit, not counting
Coarse powder / granules / ground feed ≥25 g, and expect the lab to grind and composite At 382 µm, 1% counting RSD alone needs 5.4 g after homogenisation; a single 0.2 g scoop carries ~5%
Heterogeneous blend (visible components) ≥25 g plus a note naming the minor components The minor component is the p in the formula; at p = 0.05 the error is set by how many of its particles land in the aliquot
Liquids (media, plasma, beverages) The volume the method sheet asks for, mixed, with freeze/thaw history noted No particulate term; the heterogeneity risks are settling, separation, and degradation (see Topics 16/18), not particle counting

Replicates vs mass, priced: three independent 1 g subsamples of a coarse blend (3 analyses) average the random sampling error down by √3 (5.2% → 3.0% at 0.2 g-equivalent geometry; in practice each 1 g aliquot already sits at 2.32%, and the mean of three sits near 1.34%) and, unlike one large sample, they reveal heterogeneity instead of hiding it. At $150 per analysis that trade is $450 vs $150; for a batch-release decision on a segregating blend it is usually the right spend, and for a fine homogeneous powder it is usually wasted. The arithmetic above tells you which case you are in.
