Summary: what the label math says

  • In this first audit sample (5 product archetypes, public labels only), zero labels were arithmetically impossible. Every declaration we tested can be reached from its declared ingredients. That is a finding, not a disappointment: most label math closes.
  • One archetype closes only at the very top of the published range. A whey-blend powder declaring 24 g protein per 31 g serving, with the whey blend at 93% of the formula (its own EU label), requires the blend to be 83.25% protein. Measured commercial whey protein runs 72–84% protein (Gorissen et al. 2018, Dumas nitrogen × 6.25). The label closes only if the blend is isolate-grade, with 0.75 percentage points of headroom.
  • The collagen archetype's protein number closes exactly, and its essential amino acid number does not look like protein at all. Its own published typical amino acid profile sums to 18,000 mg per serving, exactly its declared 18 g of protein, but only 2,816 mg (15.6%) of that is essential amino acids, and tryptophan is 0 mg. The same computation for whey gives 43% essential amino acids.
  • Nitrogen math is gameable by a factor you can compute on one line. One gram of glycine reads as 1.17 g of "protein" by nitrogen × 6.25; one gram of creatine monohydrate reads as 1.76 g. A hypothetical 25 g declaration containing 5 g of added glycine needs only 19.2 g of intact protein to pass a nitrogen test (worked example, §4; this is a sensitivity calculation, not a claim about any product).

So what: a protein declaration is a nitrogen statement, not an amino acid statement. Two products can both honestly print "25 g protein" and deliver very different amounts of the amino acids the buyer actually wants.

Why it matters: if you sell protein, your declaration will eventually be checked by someone: a retailer, a competitor, a plaintiff, or a customer with a lab report. The check that resolves it is not another nitrogen test. It is an amino acid analysis: total amino acids, and free amino acids, on the same sample. That pair is what separates intact protein from added nitrogen.

Introduction

A protein declaration looks like a simple promise: this serving contains this much protein. This audit asks whether that promise holds up on the label's own terms. Can the declared amount be reached from the declared ingredients at all, and what does the headline protein number hide about the amino acids the buyer actually wants?

It matters because a protein declaration is a nitrogen statement, not an amino acid statement. Two products can print the same protein number and deliver very different amounts of essential amino acids. Anyone who sells protein may eventually have that number checked by a retailer, a competitor or a customer with a lab report, and the check that settles the question is not another nitrogen test. It is an amino acid analysis, total and free, run on the same sample.

Nothing here was bought or tested. The audit works from public labels for five product archetypes, from the amino acid profile one maker publishes for its own product, and from the measured protein content and amino acid composition of commercial protein ingredients reported by Gorissen and colleagues. Three computations do the work: declared concentration, the ingredient protein fraction a declaration requires, and amino acid density per serving. Read the label facts first, then whether each declaration closes, then the amino acid density the label does not print, and finally the nitrogen lever that makes the distinction matter. Label math can flag a declaration as impossible or narrow; only a laboratory analysis of the actual product can clear or convict it.

1. How this audit works

For each archetype we take only public label facts (serving size, declared protein, ingredient list, and any amino acid profile the maker itself publishes) and three computations:

  1. Declared concentration = declared protein ÷ serving size.
  2. Required ingredient fraction = declared concentration ÷ the protein ingredient's share of the formula (where the label states that share). We compare it with the measured protein content range of that ingredient in the peer-reviewed literature.
  3. Amino acid density = expected essential amino acids (EAA) and leucine per serving, computed from the published amino acid composition of the declared protein source.

Limits, stated in the same breath as the method: label math operates on rounded label values (US labels round protein to the nearest gram), on literature ingredient ranges rather than the maker's actual ingredient lots, and on ingredient shares that most labels do not disclose. Label math can therefore flag a declaration as impossible or narrow, never as false. Where the math does not close, we say "label math that does not close" and state these limits again in that paragraph. Only a laboratory amino acid analysis of the actual product convicts or clears. In this cycle, no archetype earned that flag.

Naming rule for this series: products are described as archetypes and results are published in aggregate. Individual brands are named only in the label-facts table (§2), only for facts taken verbatim from their own public labels, and never in connection with a computed flag.

3. Computed table 1: does the declaration close?

Archetype Declared concentration Reference ingredient range (measured) Verdict from arithmetic
Whey-blend powder 24/31 = 77.4% of serving; 83.25% required of the 93% blend Whey protein: 72–84% protein across commercial samples (Gorissen 2018) Closes, narrow: requires isolate-grade blend; headroom 0.75 pts
Collagen powder 18/20 = 90.0% of serving Maker's own profile sums to 18.0 g amino acids per serving (§3, Table 2) Closes exactly, on the maker's own numbers
Plant-blend powder 21/46 = 45.7% of serving Pea isolate 77–81%, brown rice 79% protein (Gorissen 2018) Closes comfortably if the protein blend is ≥ ~57% of the formula (21 ÷ (46 × 0.80) = 57.0%); the label does not state the blend share, so this is a bound, not a verification
Milk RTD (filtered milk) 26 g / 414 ml = 6.28 g/100 ml Milk protein EAA 39% (Gorissen 2018) No concentration bound is testable from a liquid label; see Table 2
Milk-protein RTD 30 g / 325 ml = 9.23 g/100 ml Milk protein EAA 39% Same limit

Worked example (whey blend): declared concentration = 24 g ÷ 31 g = 0.7742. The blend is 93% of the formula, so the blend itself must be 0.7742 ÷ 0.93 = 0.8325, i.e. 83.25% protein. Gorissen et al. measured seven-plus commercial whey samples at 72–84% protein (nitrogen × 6.25). 83.25% sits inside that range, 0.75 points below its top. Conclusion: the declaration is achievable, and it is achievable only with a high-isolate blend. A concentrate-heavy blend at, say, 78% protein would deliver 31 × 0.93 × 0.78 = 22.5 g, which would round to 22–23 g, not 24 g. This is label math, with the limits of §1: it flags nothing about any specific tub, because we do not know any specific tub's blend. It tells a buyer which question to ask, and a brand which number to be able to prove.

Plant-blend powder 21 g 5.9–6.3 g (21 × 0.28 brown-rice basis to 21 × 0.30 pea basis) 28–30% ≈1.5 g (pea basis: 21 × 5.7/80)

Milk-protein RTD 30 g 30 × 0.39 = 11.7 g 39% 2.70 g

Two honesty notes on this table. First, the collagen profile sum of exactly 18,000 mg includes hydroxyproline (2,058 mg) and hydroxylysine (217 mg), which are real collagen constituents but are not among the standard amino acids a protein-quality calculation counts; excluding them, the standard-panel sum is 15,725 mg. Second, glycine + proline + hydroxyproline are 43.6% of the collagen profile: the 18 g is real amino acid mass, and it is mostly not the amino acids a muscle-protein buyer is paying for. Both statements come from the maker's own published profile; neither is a suspicion.

5. The nitrogen lever, computed

Protein on a label is legally nitrogen × 6.25 (21 CFR 101.9(c)(7) permits nitrogen methods). Nitrogen content is a property of each molecule, so we can compute exactly how much "protein" a gram of a cheap nitrogen source registers as:

Compound Formula / MW Nitrogen % (computed) "Protein" registered per gram (N × 6.25)
Glycine C₂H₅NO₂, 75.07 14.007/75.07 = 18.66% 1.17 g
Taurine C₂H₇NO₃S, 125.15 14.007/125.15 = 11.19% 0.70 g
Creatine monohydrate C₄H₉N₃O₂·H₂O, 149.15 3×14.007/149.15 = 28.17% 1.76 g

Sensitivity example (hypothetical, labelled as such; it describes no real product): a powder declaring 25 g protein that contained 5 g of added glycine would need only 25 − 5.83 = 19.2 g of intact protein to register 25 g by nitrogen. Its leucine, computed at whey's 11.0%, would fall from 2.75 g to 2.11 g. The total amino acid analysis would show the glycine spike directly; the free amino acid analysis would show it is added, not protein-bound. That pair of measurements is the whole resolution of the spiking question, and it costs $150 per sample, not a lawsuit.

6. Perspectives

  • Brand: self-audit with the three computations above before a retailer does. If your declaration requires the top of your ingredient's published range, keep the lot data that proves your blend sits there.
  • Retailer / co-packer: the screening rubric is cheap: declared concentration vs ingredient range, blend share disclosure, and whether a typical amino acid profile is published at all. Absence of a profile is not evidence of anything; it is a reason to test.
  • Analytical: label math flags; only measurement convicts. The discriminating measurement is total amino acids minus free amino acids, because added glycine/taurine/creatine appear in the free fraction.
  • Legal landscape: nitrogen-method protein declarations are permitted by regulation; the exposure is in marketing claims ("25 g of whey protein") that the amino acid profile may not support. This article takes no position on any product's compliance.

7. What changes next cycle

Add 2–3 archetypes per cycle (egg-white powder and a protein bar are queued); re-run the required-fraction computation when any label version changes; record brand reformulations as dated news, never silently.
