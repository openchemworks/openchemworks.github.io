Summary: key findings

  • Glutamine is the fastest mover in a neutral drink, and the matrix decides how fast. In plain water at pH 6.5 and 22–24 °C, glutamine degrades at 0.23% per day (our computed half-life: 301 days). In mixed amino-acid/dextrose and total parenteral nutrition (TPN) solutions at the same temperature, the published rate is 0.7–0.9% per day (computed half-life: 77–99 days). Same amino acid, same temperature, roughly a 4x rate difference from the matrix alone. (Khan et al. 1991; our computation, Table 1.)
  • Temperature is the biggest lever we can quantify. At 37 °C in TPN solution, glutamine loss was reported at 5% per day (computed half-life: 13.5 days). That is 6.25x the room-temperature TPN rate, and it matches the Arrhenius prediction from the published activation energy (98.7 kJ/mol at pH 6.41 predicts a 6.11x rise from 23 °C to 37 °C; Wang et al. 1999). Two independent routes land on the same number.
  • Aspartame shows the steepest pH cliff in the dataset. Half-life is about 300 days at pH 4.3 at room temperature, and only a few days at pH 7. In flavored dairy beverages (pH 6.4–6.7), measured half-lives were 24–58 days at 4 °C and 1–4 days at 30 °C. Dropping pH from 6.7 to 6.4 doubled stability. (Tsoubeli et al., J Dairy Sci 1994, as reported in the study abstract; BenchChem technical summary of the primary literature.)
  • A 12-month label claim can silently require more than double the amino acid. At the plain-water glutamine rate, 43.2% remains after 365 days at room temperature, so a product that must still deliver its label amount at month 12 needs a 131% overage at fill. At the TPN-like matrix rate, 5.3% remains, which implies a 1,776% overage. That second number is not a formulation plan. It is proof that the formulation, not the overage, has to change.
  • Gap, reported as a finding: there is no usable published rate for BCAAs in acidic beverages. We searched for leucine/isoleucine/valine degradation rates in RTD conditions and found no peer-reviewed rate constants to extract. BCAA loss in real drinks is dominated by interactions (for example Maillard chemistry with reducing sugars under heat), not by the clean first-order decay glutamine and aspartame show. Anyone quoting you a BCAA half-life in a beverage is estimating. We do not publish estimates as data.

So what: if your drink contains free glutamine or aspartame, your shelf-life claim is a kinetics problem with a computable answer, and the answer changes by an order of magnitude with pH, temperature, and matrix. Measure the fastest mover at real timepoints instead of extrapolating from a day-zero assay.

Why it matters: a release-day amino acid result tells you what you filled, not what the customer drinks at month 9. The gap between those two numbers is where label claims, retailer audits, and reformulation costs live.

Introduction

What is in the bottle on the day it is filled is not necessarily what the customer drinks months later. For drinks containing free amino acids or aspartame, the gap between those two amounts is a kinetics problem, and this article sets out to quantify it: which compounds move fastest, and how strongly temperature, pH, and the surrounding matrix change the rate.

The dataset is built only from published rates, losses per day, and half-lives reported under stated conditions by named studies, notably the glutamine work of Khan and colleagues and the aspartame work in dairy beverages. Those published figures are converted here, using the first-order arithmetic set out in the methods, into half-lives and the share remaining after set periods, so different studies can be compared on one scale.

Read the computed dataset for glutamine first, then the aspartame table, and use the perspectives that follow for what the numbers mean for formulation, quality testing, and a shelf-life claim. Where no usable published rate could be found, as for BCAAs in acidic beverages, the article reports that gap as a finding rather than filling it with an estimate.

The computed dataset

All degradation here is treated as pseudo-first-order, which is how the source studies report it: fraction remaining = exp(−k·t), half-life = ln 2 / k, and k = −ln(1 − r) where r is the published fractional loss per day.

Worked example (water row): r = 0.0023 per day. k = −ln(1 − 0.0023) = 0.00230 per day. Half-life = 0.6931 / 0.00230 = 301 days. Remaining at 365 days = exp(−0.00230 × 365) = exp(−0.841) = 0.432, or 43.2%.

pH 7.0, room temperature a few days (not precisely quantified in the sources we could verify) not computed: the source does not support a single number

Temperature scaling, computed by us from the dairy pairs: at pH 6.4 the rate rises 58 / 4 = 14.5x between 4 °C and 30 °C, which is a Q10 of 2.80 and implies an activation energy of 71.8 kJ/mol. At pH 6.7 (using range midpoints 26.5 and 1.5 days, flagged as midpoints) the rise is 17.7x, Q10 3.02, implied activation energy 77.2 kJ/mol. An independent literature value for aspartame thermal degradation, 20.77 kcal/mol (86.9 kJ/mol, from heating studies at 60–100 °C), predicts a 25.4x rise over the same 4 to 30 °C interval. The three estimates are the same order of magnitude; the heating-study value runs hotter, which is expected because it was measured in a different temperature regime and matrix. We report all three rather than averaging them into a false consensus.

Sources: Tsoubeli et al., J Dairy Sci 1994 (abstract: half-lives 1–4 days at 30 °C, 24–58 days at 4 °C, pH 6.7 to 6.4 doubling); Bell LN, ACS Symp Ser 1997 (activation energies fall as pH and moisture rise); Korean J Food Sci Technol 1996 (20.77 kcal/mol; rate constants at 100 °C by pH); BenchChem technical summary compiling the pH 4.3 / 300-day and pH 7 figures from the primary literature. Access date: November 5, 2026.

Perspectives

Formulator. The three levers rank by effect size in our tables: temperature first (a 6x glutamine rate change from 23 °C to 37 °C; a 14–18x aspartame change from 4 °C to 30 °C), matrix second (about 4x for glutamine at fixed temperature), pH third but decisive for aspartame (300 days at pH 4.3 vs a few days at pH 7). A glutamine drink that is safe at 0.23%/day in water is a different product in a nutrient-dense matrix.

QA. Test the fastest mover, not the panel average. In a glutamine-containing RTD, glutamine (and the ammonia it releases as it cyclizes to pyroglutamate) is your stability-indicating signal. A sensible timepoint grid from Table 1: day 0, 30, 90, and end of claimed shelf life, at real storage temperature, plus one accelerated arm whose extrapolation you treat as a screen, not a result.

Label. Overage math, shown: to guarantee label amount L at day 365 you must fill L / (fraction remaining). Plain-water glutamine kinetics: L / 0.432 = 2.31 × L, a 131% overage. Aspartame at its best pH (4.3): L / 0.430 = 2.32 × L. If the required overage exceeds what taste, cost, or regulation allows, the honest options are a shorter claim, a colder chain, a pH move, or a stabilized form (for glutamine, the dipeptide alanyl-glutamine exists precisely because the free form cyclizes).

Skeptic. Accelerated aging breaks where the mechanism changes: aspartame's dominant pathway switches with pH (ester hydrolysis in acid, diketopiperazine cyclization toward neutral), so a single Arrhenius line fitted at high temperature and one pH does not govern a different pH at room temperature. Our own cross-check shows the spread: implied activation energies of 71.8, 77.2, and 86.9 kJ/mol from three routes. Use accelerated data to rank formulations, and real-time data to set dates.

What to do with this

If you are setting or defending a shelf-life claim on an amino-acid drink, assay your own product at the timepoints above rather than borrowing a half-life from a different matrix.

Check your own product: Amino acid analysis, $150/sample. One AA-1 free amino acid panel per timepoint gives you the glutamine decay curve and the ammonia fingerprint of your actual formulation.

Methods & Sources

Inclusion rule: a number enters the tables only if a named published source reports a rate, a percent loss per day, or a half-life under stated pH/temperature/matrix conditions. Formulas: first-order conversion as above; Q10 = (k2/k1)^(10/(T2−T1)); Arrhenius activation energy from two rates, Ea = R·ln(k2/k1) / (1/T1 − 1/T2), T in kelvin. Limitations: (1) Khan et al. studied intravenous solutions, not commercial beverages; we use them because they are the cleanest published glutamine kinetics and we say so wherever the numbers are used. Beverage matrices with sugars, flavors, and preservatives may run faster (Maillard and catalysis) or slower. (2) The aspartame pH 7 half-life is reported only as "a few days" in verifiable sources; we did not convert it to a point value. (3) No BCAA beverage rate met the inclusion rule; that section is a reported gap, not a zero. (4) The TPN midpoint row uses 0.80%/day as the midpoint of the published band for display; the band itself is in the table.

Sources (all accessed November 5, 2026): Khan K et al., Clin Nutr 1991, 10:193–198 and companion factors paper (ScienceDirect abstracts); Wang et al., Eur J Pharm Sci 1999, 9:75–78 (activation energy 9.87×10^4 J/mol at pH 6.41; Souba 5%/day at 37 °C as cited therein); Tsoubeli et al., J Dairy Sci 1994 (ScienceDirect abstract); Bell LN, in ACS Symposium Series 675, 1997, ch. 3; Korean J Food Sci Technol 1996 (activation energy 20.77 kcal/mol). URLs in calc-notes.md.
