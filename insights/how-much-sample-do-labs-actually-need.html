How much sample do labs actually need (and why a small amount can be enough)

Published October 20, 20266 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Labs usually need less sample than people expect: the instrument measures a small prepared portion of what you send; the rest lets the lab prepare it properly, repeat a preparation if something looks off, and — where you commission it — run a spiked portion to check the matrix. For a powder, setting aside about 20 grams is a safe amount to have ready. That is guidance for what to set aside, not a universal minimum for every test or laboratory — always confirm the amount for your exact sample by email before you post it.

Why the measured portion is small

Chromatography — the separation technique behind the test described here — works on small, carefully prepared solutions. In AQC (AccQ-Tag) amino acid analysis with HPLC-UV, a portion of your sample is dissolved or extracted, the amino acids are chemically tagged so they can be detected, and a small volume of that prepared solution is what actually passes through the instrument. The 19 amino acids plus ammonia in the free panel, AA-1, are measured from that preparation against a calibrated standard. Cysteine, for reference, is not part of that free panel; it needs a separate AA-3 workup, which is one more reason to describe your sample and your question before assuming one parcel covers every possible test.

If the instrument consumes so little, why send more? Because measurement is the last step, not the only step. Everything before it — weighing, dissolving a powder evenly, diluting a strong or salty sample into range, filtering or clarifying a sauce — consumes sample too, and each step must be done well or the small final portion will faithfully misrepresent your product.

What the extra sample is actually for

First, representativeness. A blend that is not perfectly mixed differs at the top of the jar from the bottom. A slightly larger sample, taken from a well-mixed batch, lets the lab prepare a portion that stands for the batch, not one scoop. The most elegant analysis of an unrepresentative scoop is still a report about the wrong material.

Second, preparation repeats. If a preparation looks anomalous — a result that does not fit the profile, a visible preparation problem — the honest response is to prepare again from retained sample and compare, rather than report a single suspect number. That is only possible if enough sample arrived and was retained. It is also why this laboratory reruns an unexpected result before reporting it where the situation calls for it, rather than printing the first trace and moving on.

Third, qualification work. Matrix-effect qualification, where commissioned, needs extra portions of your actual sample: one prepared normally, one with a known spike of the analyte added, so recovery can be measured. A dry seasoning is the classic case — its matrix of salt, colour, and spice can distort a reading, and checking that is impossible without sample to spare. Qualification is priced per sample and matrix on inquiry; a recent single-sample seasoning project with full matrix-effect qualification was $350.

Fourth, form. Liquids and powders behave differently in preparation. A powder must be dissolved evenly; a sauce may need homogenising — thorough mixing so the portion taken reflects the whole — and may separate in transit. The amount for your form is part of the quote conversation, not a detail to improvise at the post office.

The 20-gram guidance, stated precisely

For a powder, ask for about 20 grams to set aside. That is this laboratory's guidance on record and a sensible planning figure: small enough for a modest sealed container, large enough to cover preparation, a repeat, and qualification portions where commissioned. Do not generalise it carelessly. It is not stated here as a minimum below which testing is impossible, nor as the amount every other laboratory requires, nor as the right amount for every liquid, sauce, or total-analysis order. Total amino acid analysis, AA-T, hydrolyses the sample in 6 N hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 hours before measurement, at $230 per sample, and its preparation needs are part of its own confirmation. The AA-1 panel itself costs $150 per sample for liquids, $180 per sample for powders ($150 plus $30 powder preparation), $150 flat for first-time customers, and $125 per sample at 25 or more samples per month.

The correct sequence is: email the lab, describe the sample, its form, and your question, receive confirmation of the amount and total price, then weigh and send. If you have very little of a precious batch, say so in that first email — the lab can tell you what is feasible rather than you discovering a shortfall after posting. If you have plenty, do not send a kilo hoping for a better result. More material does not improve a measurement limited by preparation and matrix, not quantity, and oversized parcels simply cost more to ship to Davis, California.

Sending it well

Use a clean, dry, sealed container for a powder, labelled inside and out with your name and a sample description matching your email. Keep the batch identity: note the recipe version and date mixed, for your own records, so the report can be filed against the right batch. Send the batch you mean to describe — well mixed, as sold or as eaten, not a special laboratory-only preparation no customer will ever meet. Your next step

Before you weigh anything, email info@openchemworks.com with what the sample is, whether it is a liquid or powder, and your one-sentence question. Ask for the confirmed amount and the all-in price. Then set aside the confirmed amount — for a typical powder, expect around the 20-gram guidance above — and post it only after the reply.

Frequently asked questions

Can I send less than 20 grams of a powder?
Ask first. Twenty grams is safe planning guidance, not a stated absolute minimum. If your batch is small, describe the situation and let the lab confirm what it can do.

Why does the lab not just ask for the tiny amount the instrument uses?
Because preparation, representativeness, repeats, and any spike-recovery work all consume sample before and beside the instrument portion. The measured volume is only the end of the process.

Does sending more sample make the result more accurate?
No. Beyond enough for good preparation and repeats, accuracy is governed by mixing, preparation, calibration, and matrix behaviour — not by parcel weight.

Is the amount different for a sauce?
Liquids are handled and priced differently from powders, and a sauce's amount is confirmed per enquiry. Do not assume the powder guidance transfers by weight or volume.
