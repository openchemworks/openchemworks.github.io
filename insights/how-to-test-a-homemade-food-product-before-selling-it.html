How to test a homemade food product before selling it

Published October 20, 2026Updated November 19, 20268 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Test the exact batch you plan to sell from, in the form you plan to sell it, before you print a label. For amino acid or glutamate claims that is a free amino acid analysis (AA-1, $150 per sample); for claims about the protein itself it is total amino acids (AA-T, $230). Testing supports honest claims. It does not make a product legal or safe to sell, and it does not replace the food-safety and licensing steps your state requires.

The moment this page is for

You have a product people ask to buy: a seasoning, a sauce, a rub, a drink mix or a baked good that started in your kitchen. You are pricing labels or building a listing, and you reach the sentence where you describe what is in it. That sentence is the risk. Write "high in protein," imply a glutamate level, or print an amino acid figure you estimated from an ingredient database, and you are making a claim a customer, retailer or regulator can test against the jar in front of them. Databases describe average ingredients, not your batch, your supplier or your mixing.

There is a quieter version of the same job. You scale a recipe or change a supplier and the taste shifts, and you cannot tell whether the shift is real. Without a measured baseline for the batch that tasted right, you have only memory to compare against.

What testing can and cannot do

Testing measures composition: how much of each measured amino acid is present, free as eaten (AA-1) or total after the protein is broken apart (AA-T). It cannot assess microbial safety, shelf life, allergen cross-contact or licensing. Treat any seller guide that bundles "one test and you are ready to sell" with suspicion.

It helps to sort claims into three groups before you print.

  • Claims a free amino acid report can back: a named amino acid was measured at a stated amount in the tested product, on the same basis as the report. Name the amino acid, say "free," copy the basis exactly and keep the batch identity with the report. Free glutamate can be stated as an MSG equivalent only if the report names the form used (anhydrous or monohydrate). Consistency language is fine in modest form: if you test successive batches the same way, you can say so.
  • Claims that need the total test or different wording: anything about the amino acids in the protein belongs to AA-T. AA-T reports 16 amino acids; tryptophan is destroyed in hydrolysis, cysteine is not reported (it needs an AA-3 workup), and asparagine and glutamine are reported with aspartate and glutamate. No report from AA-1 supports "complete amino acid profile." Say "measured for 19 free amino acids" instead. Do not let a designer round a measured value into a rounder claim, and do not re-express a per-100 g result per serving unless the serving really is 100 g.
  • Claims this testing cannot back: safety, health or function claims, quality superlatives and regulatory compliance. An OpenChemWorks report will not make them for you.

Free or total, and what "MSG in a blend" means

Most first-time small sellers need the free panel. AA-1 covers 19 amino acids plus ammonia, measured by AQC (AccQ-Tag) derivatization with HPLC and dual UV and fluorescence detection. Cysteine is not in the panel and needs the AA-3 workup. Total analysis (AA-T) first hydrolyses the sample in 6 N hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 hours, so it cannot tell you how much glutamate was free for taste.

When people ask "how much MSG is in this?" they mean one of three things, and a lab can answer only the one you name. Added MSG is how much MSG powder went into the recipe: only your recipe record answers that, because no test can separate added MSG from glutamate in tomato powder or yeast extract. Free glutamate is all the glutamate present in free form, from every source combined, and it is usually the number that matters for savoury taste. Total glutamate adds the glutamate released from protein, always reads higher, and includes glutamine converted to glutamate. Read free vs total glutamate before you order, and see how to read the units when the report arrives.

Blends and sauces are hard to measure

A dry blend is a mixture whose proportions vary scoop to scoop if mixing is uneven, and its strongest components (salt, chilli, dark spices, dried mushroom or tomato, yeast powders) dominate its character. A sauce adds water, oil, acid and cooking. In both, the matrix, everything in the sample besides the amino acids, can pull a reading off true.

A matrix check settles that. A known amount of glutamate is added to a portion of your actual sample and the lab measures how much it recovers. Good recovery means the matrix leaves the reading alone; poor recovery shows the size and direction of the distortion. This is PREP-2, new-matrix qualification, $200 per assay, once per formulation, waived at 20 or more samples. It matters most for salty, dark or oily products and is not needed for every simple liquid.

Your next step

Write down the exact claim sentence you want to use. Then email info@openchemworks.com with that sentence, what the product is, and whether it is liquid or powder. The reply tells you whether a free or total test backs that sentence, whether matrix qualification is advised, and the all-in price. If the honest answer is that amino acid testing does not back your sentence, change the sentence. That is the test doing its job before a customer does it for you. Pre-print check: does the report name this amino acid, use the same fraction (free or total), use the same basis and MSG form, and cover the product as sold from a batch you can identify? Six yeses and the claim matches its evidence.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need testing before I can legally sell homemade food?
This page cannot answer that. Licensing and food-safety rules vary by state and product and sit outside amino acid analysis. Check your state's cottage-food rules and your market's requirements separately.

Should I test every batch?
That depends on how variable your ingredients and mixing are and how specific your claim is. Test at least the recipe you launch with, and retest when the recipe or supplier changes.

Can I print the lab result on my label?
What you may print depends on labelling rules for your claim. Send us the claim wording and we will tell you what our report can support before you print. We are not yet accredited, so if a retailer or regulator requires an accredited lab, we are not the right fit.

What if I only sell a sauce, not a dry blend?
A sauce is tested as a liquid: AA-1 is $150 per sample with no powder preparation charge, and the same free versus total choice applies.

Can a lab tell me how much MSG I added to my blend?
No. The test measures free glutamate from all ingredients combined, such as tomato powder, yeast extract and mushroom powder. Only your recipe record shows the added portion.
