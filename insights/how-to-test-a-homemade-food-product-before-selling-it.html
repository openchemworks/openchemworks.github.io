How to test a homemade or small-batch food product before selling it

Published October 20, 20266 min readOpenChemWorks Laboratory
Short answer

Before you sell a homemade or small-batch food product, test the claims you intend to make — starting with one sample of the exact batch you will sell from. If you plan to talk about protein, amino acids, or glutamate, a free amino acid analysis of that sample tells you whether the numbers behind your words are real. It will not, on its own, make a product legal or safe to sell: testing supports honest claims; it does not replace the food-safety and licensing steps your state and market require.

The moment this page is for

You have a product people ask to buy. Maybe it is a seasoning, a sauce, a drink mix, or a baked good that started in your kitchen. You are pricing labels, booking a market stall, or building a simple online listing, and you reach the sentence where you describe what is in it.

That sentence is the risk. Write "high in protein" or imply a glutamate level or print an amino acid figure you estimated from an ingredient database, and you are making a claim a customer, a retailer, or a regulator can test against the jar in front of them. Ingredient databases describe average ingredients, not your batch, your supplier, your grind, or your mixing. The job, then, is not "get a lab report" in the abstract. It is: know what is actually in the batch I am about to sell, before I put words on it.

What testing can do for that job — and what it cannot

Testing can measure composition: how much of each measured amino acid is present, in free form or, with a different test, in total after the protein is broken down. It can confirm a blend is consistent with its ingredient list, show you the free glutamate behind a savoury claim, and give you a defensible number for your own recipe decisions.

Testing of this kind cannot assess microbial safety, shelf life, allergen cross-contact in your kitchen, or whether your home production is licensed in your state. Those are separate questions with separate experts and rules, and no amino acid result answers them. Treat any seller guide that bundles "one test and you are ready to sell" with suspicion. This page covers only the composition-and-claims slice this laboratory performs.

It also cannot certify you. OpenChemWorks is not GLP, GMP, or ISO/IEC 17025 accredited. Its results are for the maker's own decisions, not regulatory submission. For many cottage and small-batch sellers, a composition result for your own claim-checking is exactly what you need. If a farmers market, retailer, or platform asks you for an accredited report or a specific named test, get that requirement in writing and match it exactly before paying any lab.

The measurement you have been missing

For amino-acid-related claims, the measurement is amino acid analysis. Two versions matter, and choosing the wrong one wastes your money.

Free amino acid analysis measures the amino acids already free in the product as eaten. The panel here, AA-1, covers 19 amino acids plus ammonia, measured by AQC (AccQ-Tag) derivatization with HPLC-UV — the amino acids are tagged, separated, and measured against a calibrated standard. Cysteine is excluded from the free panel and needs a separate AA-3 workup. Free analysis suits questions about taste-active glutamate, the free profile of a sauce or blend, and whether a "free amino acid" statement you want to make is true.

Total amino acid analysis, AA-T, first hydrolyses the sample in 6 N hydrochloric acid at 110 °C for 24 hours, releasing amino acids bound in protein, then measures them. It suits questions about the protein's overall makeup. It will not tell you how much glutamate was free for taste, because hydrolysis frees the bound fraction too.

Most first-time small sellers who write to a lab like this one need the free panel, plus honest wording. If your product is a seasoning blend, ask also about matrix-effect qualification: the blend itself — salt, colour, spice oils — can distort a reading, and qualification checks that distortion with a known spike in your actual sample. It is priced per sample and matrix on inquiry; a recent single-sample seasoning project with full matrix-effect qualification was $350.

Test the batch you will sell from, mixed the way you will sell it. A result describes the sample tested; a new supplier, recipe change, or noticeably different batch deserves a new check if the claim stays on the label. Set aside sample only in the amount the lab confirms — email first, describe the product and its form, and confirm before posting.

Your next step

Write down the exact claim sentence you want to use. Then email info@openchemworks.com with that sentence, what the product is, and whether it is liquid or powder. The reply should tell you whether a free or total test backs that sentence, whether matrix qualification is advised, and the all-in price. If the honest answer is that amino acid testing does not back your sentence, change the sentence — that is the test doing its job before a customer does it for you.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need testing before I can legally sell homemade food?
This page cannot answer that — licensing and food-safety rules vary by state and product and sit outside amino acid analysis. Check your state's cottage-food rules and your market's requirements separately.

Should I test every batch?
That depends on how variable your ingredients and mixing are and how specific your claim is. Test at least the recipe you launch with, and retest when the recipe or supplier changes.

Can I print the lab result on my label?
A result for your own decisions is not a regulatory submission document. What you may print depends on labelling rules for your claim — get the claim wording checked against the reporting-bases guidance linked below.

What if I only sell a sauce, not a dry blend?
Liquids are tested at $150 per sample for AA-1, and the same free-versus-total choice applies.
