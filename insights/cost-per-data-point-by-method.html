Summary: key findings

Every price below is a published price, verified on the lab's own page on November 5, 2026, except the OpenChemWorks rows and the census seed rows, which come from the Insights program plan's verified seed (plan §1, Topic 1, verified 2026-11-05). Cost per analyte is our computation: published price ÷ analytes reported. Where a lab does not publish its analyte count, we say so and show the scenario, because that missing number is itself a finding.

  • The spread in cost per analyte is 14x across published offerings. Cheapest per reported analyte in this census: Walk-In Lab's LC/MS blood panel at $255 ÷ 36 listed analytes = $7.08 per analyte. Most expensive per analyte: UC Davis's single-analyte service at $101.00 for exactly one analyte. In between: OpenChemWorks AA-1 at $7.50, UC Davis plasma panel at $9.40 (on a 20-analyte denominator; see the gap note), Bio-Synthesis modified/blank analysis at $16.67, Medallion ion-exchange profile at $19.12, Bio-Synthesis hydrolysis + analysis at $25.00.
  • The hydrolysis-only trap: $200 buys zero data points. Bio-Synthesis charges $200/sample for hydrolysis only (hydrolysate returned to you). Cost per analyte is not high, it is undefined: $200 ÷ 0 reported analytes. It is a preparation service, not an analysis, and any per-analyte comparison that quietly includes it is broken. We list it precisely so the distinction is on the record.
  • What you pay for hydrolysis-inclusive answers differs by 1.7x. Bio-Synthesis standard HCl hydrolysis + analysis: $450 for 18 quantified amino acids (their sample-preparation page states the service quantifies 18 of the 20 standard amino acids, excluding tryptophan and cysteine) = $25.00/analyte. OpenChemWorks AA-T (total): $230 for 16 reported amino acids (denominator: 16 amino acids, the site's published total-amino-acid panel) = $14.38/analyte. Medallion (cation-exchange, post-column ninhydrin, AOAC 982.30 modified): $325, with cystine, methionine, and tryptophan explicitly not quantified, so at most 17 of 20 = $19.12/analyte on the generous denominator.
  • Panel denominator honesty changes our own number too. OpenChemWorks AA-1 reports 19 amino acids + ammonia = 20 reported values (denominator stated explicitly), so $150 ÷ 20 = $7.50/analyte. An earlier draft variant used 18 amino acids + ammonia (19 values, $7.89); that variant is removed by the November 5, 2026 science check because it contradicts the site's published AA-1 panel (19 amino acids + ammonia). The ranking does not change; the arithmetic is the article.
  • Turnaround is a second price. Cost per business day of stated turnaround (price ÷ midpoint days, our computation): Bio-Synthesis $450 ÷ 6 = $75.00/day; OpenChemWorks AA-T $230 ÷ 4 = $57.50/day; AA-1 $150 ÷ 4 = $37.50/day; Medallion $325 ÷ 13.5 = $24.07/day. Medallion is the cheapest per day and the slowest in absolute days (12–15 business days). UC Davis publishes no turnaround on its rates page, so no per-day figure exists for it. That absence is reported, not filled.
  • Coverage is the third price. Against a 22-item reference set (20 proteinogenic amino acids + ammonia + taurine), published-panel coverage computes to: Walk-In LC/MS list 21/22 = 95.5% (all 20 plus taurine; ammonia not listed); OpenChemWorks AA-1 panel 20/22 = 90.9% (19 amino acids + ammonia; taurine not in the panel description); Bio-Synthesis hydrolysis service 18/22 = 81.8%; Medallion 17/22 = 77.3%. UC Davis's panel contents are not enumerated on its rates page, so its coverage score is not computable from published data. For a university reference laboratory, that is a surprising gap, and it is the single most useful question a buyer can ask them.

So what: "price per sample" hides three separate purchases: how many values come back, how fast, and which analytes are silently missing. Normalize all three before comparing quotes, and ask any lab that will not publish its analyte list why not.

Why it matters: the missing analytes are rarely random. Hydrolysis destroys tryptophan and damages cysteine and methionine, so total panels differ in exactly the amino acids that are often first-limiting or label-critical. A cheap panel that omits your limiting amino acid is not cheap.

Introduction

A price per sample tells a buyer almost nothing on its own, because panels do not return the same number of values, do not cover the same analytes, and do not report back at the same speed. This article asks the buyer’s real question: for each published amino acid offering, what does one reported data point actually cost, and what else is being bought, or quietly left out, alongside it?

Every price here is a published price, verified on the lab’s own page, and the central figure is a single transparent computation: published price divided by the number of analytes reported. Two further divisions sit alongside it, price against stated turnaround and coverage against a reference set of analytes. Where a lab does not publish its analyte count, we say so and show the assumption rather than filling the gap, because that missing denominator is itself part of what a buyer needs to know.

Read the census table with its warnings attached. A preparation service that reports no analytes has no cost per analyte at all, however it is priced, and a long analyte list only helps if it includes the amino acids your decision turns on. The perspectives that follow take the same numbers from the buyer’s, the analyst’s and the skeptic’s side, so the cheapest row is never mistaken for the right buy for every job.

Two denominator warnings that are part of the dataset, not footnotes to hide: (1) Bio-Synthesis's service page headline says "up to 42 amino acids and related compounds supported" across its platform. On that denominator the hydrolysis service would compute to $450 ÷ 42 = $10.71. We use 18 because their own sample-preparation page states what the standard hydrolysis service quantifies. A buyer should confirm in writing which denominator a quote uses. (2) Hydrolysis reporting lines: in a total (hydrolyzed) panel, asparagine reports as aspartic acid (Asx) and glutamine as glutamic acid (Glx), so the count of distinct reported lines is smaller than the count of amino acids in the sample. AA-T's denominator is 16 reported amino acids, as published for the total panel in Topic 1; if your decision depends on distinct lines, ask for the report template, ours included.

Perspectives

Buyer. Three defined jobs. (1) "Is my product's amino acid profile what the label says?" A free-amino-acid panel on the finished product: AA-1 class, $7.50 per reported value, 3–5 days. (2) "What is the total protein quality of this ingredient?" A hydrolysis-inclusive panel: $14.38–$25.00 per analyte across this census; the buying question is whether Trp/Cys/Met handling is specified, because that is where the panels silently diverge. (3) "One number, defensibly: taurine in this plasma." A single-analyte service at $101 is the most expensive row in Table 1 per analyte and may still be the right buy, because you are purchasing a reference laboratory's validated single-analyte method, not 1/20th of a panel. Per-analyte cost ranks efficiency; it does not rank fitness for a job.

Analytical. What the extra money buys, honestly bounded: ion-exchange with post-column ninhydrin (UC Davis, Medallion) is the long-standing reference approach, robust for physiological fluids and food hydrolysates, at the cost of long run times and, at Medallion as published, no Cys/Met/Trp. AccQ-Tag UPLC/HPLC (Bio-Synthesis, OpenChemWorks) buys speed and small sample volume with pre-column derivatization; its hydrolysis caveats for Trp/Cys are the same chemistry, not a brand difference. LC-MS/MS (Walk-In's clinical panel) buys the broadest metabolite list per dollar in this census, aimed at blood diagnostics, not at food or feed matrices. Sensitivity claims beyond what these pages publish are not compared here, because they were not published in comparable form. That is a gap in the public record, not a tie.

Lab economics. Ion-exchange costs what it costs because the instrument is dedicated, runs are long, and the method carries decades of validation in regulated matrices; you are paying for defensibility per run, amortized slowly. Derivatization HPLC/UPLC spreads a general-purpose instrument across many assay types, which is part of why per-analyte prices cluster lower. None of that makes one "better"; it explains the price structure a buyer is looking at.

Skeptic. Where LC-MS/MS is overkill: a 36-analyte clinical blood list is wasted on a feed ingredient whose specification turns on 6 amino acids, and its per-analyte crown in Table 1 is partly an artifact of counting metabolites a food buyer never asked for. Where HPLC-UV is not enough: trace free amino acids in a dilute beverage, or any job where a co-eluting peak would change a label decision; there, mass-selective detection earns its premium. And the standing skeptic note for this whole article: per-analyte arithmetic rewards long lists. Coverage against the analytes your decision actually needs (Table 1, last column, and your own specification) is the correction.

What to do with this

Normalize any quote you receive with the three divisions above before you compare it to anything, including to us.

Check your own product: Amino acid analysis, $150/sample. AA-1 free amino acid analysis, AccQ-Tag on HPLC, priced on this page at $7.50 per reported value (20 reported values: 19 amino acids + ammonia), 3–5 business days.
