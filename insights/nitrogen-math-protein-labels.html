At a glance: what the numbers say

  • 1 gram of glycine reads as 1.17 grams of "protein" on a nitrogen test. Glycine (C2H5NO2, molecular weight 75.07) is 18.66% nitrogen. 0.1866 g nitrogen × 6.25 = 1.166 g of apparent protein. Glycine is not protein. A nitrogen test cannot tell the difference.
  • 1 gram of creatine monohydrate reads as 1.76 grams of "protein." Creatine monohydrate is 28.17% nitrogen (three nitrogen atoms), so 1 g contributes 0.2817 × 6.25 = 1.761 g of apparent protein, more leverage per gram than any common spiking agent except urea.
  • To fake 8.5 g of protein you need only 7.3 g of glycine, or 4.8 g of creatine monohydrate. 8.5 / 1.166 = 7.29 g glycine. 8.5 / 1.761 = 4.83 g creatine monohydrate. 8.5 g is the amount the Body Fortress class-action complaint alleged was spiked into a serving labeled 30 g of protein (21.5 g alleged true whey protein; 8.5 / 30 = 28.3% of the claim).
  • Honest ingredients nearly close under amino-acid-sum math; spiked math does not. Summing all 18 amino acids published by USDA FoodData Central for soy protein isolate gives 88.08 g per 100 g against 88.32 g of nitrogen protein, a gap of −0.3%. For wheat flour the gap is 0.0%. The gaps that remain for honest ingredients are small, explainable (see the gelatin finding below), and stable. Added free nitrogen breaks the closure in one direction only: nitrogen protein rises, amino acid sum does not.
  • Gelatin is the exception that proves the method needs care: its amino acid sum is 76.53 g against 85.6 g of nitrogen protein, a −10.6% gap, and the gap is a database gap, not spiking. The FoodData Central gelatin entry publishes no hydroxyproline value, and hydroxyproline is a major collagen amino acid. Any audit that flags gelatin on sum math alone, without reading the source entry, is wrong. We flag it here, in our own table, on purpose.
  • So what: If your product's nitrogen protein and its amino-acid-sum protein disagree by more than a few percent, in the direction nitrogen > sum, you have either added free nitrogen, a database hole like gelatin's, or a labeling problem. Two tests settle which: a free amino acid profile (AA-1) and a total amino acid profile (AA-T). Total minus free is the protein-bound fraction. Nitrogen alone can never give you that split.
  • Why it matters: The 2014 CRN/AHPA voluntary guidance already tells the industry to exclude non-protein nitrogen from protein declarations, retailer verification programs test for exactly this, and the Body Fortress litigation shows the allegation alone is expensive. A $150 free profile plus a $230 total profile ($380 for the pair) is the cheapest way to know your own number before a retailer, a plaintiff, or a competitor computes it for you.

Introduction

A protein declaration is usually not measured as protein at all. It is measured as nitrogen and multiplied by 6.25, on the assumption that every nitrogen atom in the sample arrived inside a protein. This article asks how much of the protein printed on a label that assumption can manufacture: how many grams of apparent protein a gram of free glycine, taurine, or creatine adds to a nitrogen result, and how far a finished product can drift from its label before anyone measuring amino acids would notice.

The question matters to anyone who buys, formulates, or sells a protein product, because the gap is not theoretical. It is the mechanism behind protein-spiking litigation, behind retailer verification programs, and behind raw materials that can be priced as protein while delivering mostly cheap free nitrogen. If your product is honest, the same arithmetic is your defense, provided you have run it on your own formulation before someone else does.

Everything here is computed, and every input is named. The exchange rates come from molecular formulas and the nitrogen fraction of each compound. The closure test sums the amino acids published by USDA FoodData Central for honest ingredients and sets that sum against their nitrogen protein. The closing scenario applies the exchange rates to a stated label claim. Read the tables in that order: first what each gram of added nitrogen is worth, then whether honest ingredients close, then what a stated addition does to a real label. The free and total amino acid profiles are the pair of measurements that settle which side of the gap any actual product sits on.

Table 1: The spiking exchange rates (computed from molecular formulas)

Nitrogen methods (Kjeldahl, Dumas) measure total nitrogen and multiply by 6.25, the factor 21 CFR 101.9(c)(7) permits for protein declaration. Every nitrogen atom counts, wherever it sits.

Compound Formula Molecular weight Nitrogen % by weight Apparent "protein" per 1 g added (g) Grams needed to fake 8.5 g protein
Glycine C2H5NO2 75.07 18.66 1.166 7.29
Taurine C2H7NO3S 125.15 11.19 0.700 12.15
Creatine (anhydrous) C4H9N3O2 131.13 32.05 2.003 4.24
Creatine monohydrate C4H9N3O2·H2O 149.15 28.17 1.761 4.83
Urea (reference, not a food ingredient claim) CH4N2O 60.06 46.64 2.915 2.92
True protein (the 6.25 assumption) — — 16.00 (assumed) 1.000 8.50

Worked example. Glycine nitrogen fraction = 14.007 / 75.07 = 0.1866. Apparent protein per gram = 0.1866 × 6.25 = 1.166 g. Grams of glycine to mimic 8.5 g protein = 8.5 / 1.166 = 7.29 g. Creatine monohydrate: nitrogen mass = 3 × 14.007 = 42.021; fraction = 42.021 / 149.15 = 0.2817; × 6.25 = 1.761 g apparent protein per gram; 8.5 / 1.761 = 4.83 g. Every figure in Table 1 re-performs from the formula and standard atomic weight of nitrogen (14.007) alone.

Ingredient (FDC ID, SR Legacy) Protein, N × 6.25 (g) Sum of 18 published amino acids (g) Gap (g) Gap (% of N protein)

Wheat flour, white, all-purpose (169761) 10.33 10.33 0.00 0.0
Egg, whole, dried (172188) 48.05 48.51 +0.46 +1.0
Milk, dry, nonfat (172195) 36.16 37.98 +1.82 +5.0
Soy flour, full-fat, raw (174273) 37.81 36.53 −1.28 −3.4
Whey, sweet, dried (171283) 12.93 12.33 −0.60 −4.6
Peas, green split, raw (172428) 23.12 21.65 −1.47 −6.4
Gelatins, dry powder (169599) 85.60 76.53 −9.07 −10.6 (see note)

Worked example. Soy protein isolate: sum = 1.116 (Trp) + 3.137 (Thr) + 4.253 (Ile) + 6.783 (Leu) + 5.327 (Lys) + 1.130 (Met) + 1.046 (Cys) + 4.593 (Phe) + 3.222 (Tyr) + 4.098 (Val) + 6.670 (Arg) + 2.303 (His) + 3.589 (Ala) + 10.203 (Asp) + 17.452 (Glu) + 3.603 (Gly) + 4.960 (Pro) + 4.593 (Ser) = 88.08 g. Gap = 88.08 − 88.32 = −0.24 g; −0.24 / 88.32 = −0.3%. Full per-amino-acid values are in data.csv.

Reading the signs: a small positive gap (egg, milk) is expected, because FoodData Central amino acids are weighed as free amino acids after hydrolysis, and hydrolysis adds a water molecule across every peptide bond, so the free-amino-acid sum slightly overstates residue mass. A small negative gap reflects amino acids not listed, rounding, and non-amino-acid nitrogen in the food. A large positive nitrogen-minus-sum gap in a finished powder, with no database hole to explain it, is the spiking signature. Gelatin's large gap has a named explanation (no hydroxyproline in the entry) and is therefore a caution about the method, not an accusation.

Table 3: What a stated addition does to a 30 g label claim (computed scenario)

Scenario, stated in full so it can be challenged: a serving contains 21.5 g of true whey protein. Add free compounds. The nitrogen declaration is recomputed from Table 1 exchange rates.

Addition to the 21.5 g true protein Apparent protein declared (g) True protein (g) Share of declaration that is not protein (%)
None 21.50 21.5 0.0
+ 7.29 g glycine 30.00 21.5 28.3
+ 4.83 g creatine monohydrate 30.00 21.5 28.3
+ 12.15 g taurine 30.00 21.5 28.3
+ 5.00 g glycine (partial) 27.33 21.5 21.3

Worked example. 21.5 + (7.29 × 1.166) = 21.5 + 8.50 = 30.00 g declared. Non-protein share = 8.50 / 30.00 = 28.3%. Partial case: 5.00 × 1.166 = 5.83; 21.5 + 5.83 = 27.33 g declared; (27.33 − 21.5) / 27.33 = 21.3% of the declaration is glycine nitrogen wearing a protein label.

This scenario is arithmetic, not a measurement of any product on sale. It is calibrated to the Body Fortress complaint's allegation (30 g claimed, 21.5 g alleged whey protein) to show that the alleged gap is achievable with single-digit grams of cheap free compounds. An allegation is not a finding; the complaint is cited for its numbers, not as a verdict.

Perspectives

Regulatory: permitted method, narrowing guidance

21 CFR 101.9(c)(7) permits protein to be calculated from nitrogen. FDA's position, restated in guidance and warning contexts, is that protein should reflect actual protein. The Council for Responsible Nutrition and AHPA 2014 voluntary guidance tells manufacturers not to count non-protein nitrogen toward protein. The gap between "the method permits it" and "the guidance forbids it" is where litigation lives.

Analytical: why the total-minus-free pair resolves it

Kjeldahl and Dumas return one number: total nitrogen. They are structurally blind to spiking. Amino acid analysis returns two: the free profile before hydrolysis (AA-1) and the total profile after hydrolysis (AA-T). Free glycine, taurine, and creatine appear in the free run at full strength and add nothing bound; protein-bound amino acids appear only after hydrolysis. Total minus free, amino acid by amino acid, is the bound protein profile, and its sum is a protein estimate no free addition can inflate.

Commercial: the cost asymmetry

Defending or settling a spiking claim, reformulating under retailer pressure, or losing a listing each cost orders of magnitude more than verification. The verification pair is $150 + $230 = $380 per product. Table 1 is also the procurement warning in reverse: if a protein raw material is priced as if its nitrogen were all protein, you may be buying glycine at whey prices.

Formulator: build a product whose math closes

Declare protein from ingredients whose Table 2 closure you have checked. If you add glycine, taurine, or creatine for a functional reason, disclose them outside the protein line, keep the addition and its nitrogen contribution (Table 1) in the formulation record, and verify the finished product once with the total-minus-free pair. A product whose math closes has nothing to fear from anyone else's arithmetic, including this page's.
